## Phase 1: Data Acquisition & Consolidation

### Provenance of the raw data

The empirical basis of this study is a corpus of primary public-sector expenditure disclosures rather than a pre-packaged research dataset. It is assembled from two structurally different classes of source.

The first class is trust-level "spend over £25,000" transparency data, published monthly by individual NHS bodies under the Freedom of Information Act 2000 and the associated Local Government Transparency Code obligations that apply to public expenditure disclosure. Three publishing bodies were selected to give variation in organisational scale and geography while keeping the disclosure format broadly comparable: Bradford Teaching Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust (an acute teaching trust), United Lincolnshire Hospitals NHS Trust (a large multi-site rural acute trust), and NHS England together with its commissioning support units (a national commissioning body, included to provide a high-volume national comparator against which trust-level behaviour can be contextualised). Each body publishes one file per calendar month, and for the six financial years spanning 2019 to 2024 this yields exactly 72 monthly snapshots per body — 216 monthly files in total. The choice of a 2019–2024 window is deliberate and central to the research design: it provides approximately fourteen months of pre-pandemic baseline, the acute COVID-19 procurement period, and a substantial post-acute recovery window, which is the minimum span required to distinguish a durable shift in procurement behaviour from a transient shock.

The second class is the UK Government's Contracts Finder bulk data export, which publishes contract notices and award records for all central government, local government, and wider public-sector procurement above the relevant thresholds, in the Open Contracting Data Standard (OCDS) relational form. This is a *national*, cross-sector export rather than a health-specific one, a property whose methodological consequences are discussed at length below. Three of its constituent relations were used — the notice-level table (`main.csv`), the award-level table (`awards.csv`), and the award-to-supplier bridging table (`awards_suppliers.csv`) — across the same six years, giving 18 files.

The complete archive as hosted comprises 443 files across the four source folders, in a mixture of comma-separated (293), Excel `.xlsx` (83), legacy Excel `.xls` (60), macro-enabled Excel `.xlsm` (1), and gzip-compressed (6) formats. Many of these are duplicate re-publications of the same month in a second format, or supplementary files (quarterly summaries, data dictionaries) not required here; the acquisition stage therefore selects a canonical 234-file working set — 72 monthly files per trust source plus the 18 Contracts Finder relations — totalling approximately 613 MB.

### Reproducibility as a design constraint

The raw archive cannot be redistributed inside the project's version-control repository. Its size (613 MB) exceeds what is reasonable to commit, and the Contracts Finder extracts are third-party Crown-copyright material subject to their own licensing terms. At the same time, a dissertation whose empirical claims rest on a locally-held folder of spreadsheets that no examiner can regenerate is not meaningfully reproducible, and the credibility of every downstream statistic depends on the acquisition step being auditable.

The design adopted resolves this tension by hosting the archive in a public, link-accessible Google Drive folder and treating acquisition as an explicit, versioned, automated pipeline stage rather than as undocumented manual preparation. A dedicated module (`src/data_engineering/build_raw_from_drive.py`) enumerates the remote folder, downloads only the canonical working set, and consolidates it into the four per-source files that the rest of the pipeline consumes. The stage is idempotent and resumable — already-downloaded files are skipped, so an interrupted acquisition can simply be re-run — and it is wired into the pipeline orchestrator conditionally, executing only when the consolidated outputs are absent. A researcher with no prior setup can therefore reproduce the entire study, acquisition included, with a single command; a researcher who already holds the data pays no cost.

Two aspects of this arrangement are limitations that should be stated rather than concealed. First, anonymous access to the hosting service is rate-limited: bulk retrieval of the full archive triggers throttling, which the module handles with bounded exponential retry and a fallback transport, but which makes a cold acquisition run take several minutes and, in principle, makes it dependent on a third-party service's continued availability. Second, hosting a mirror is not the same as citing an authoritative archive; the mirror is a convenience for reproduction, and the primary sources remain the publishing bodies' own transparency pages and the Contracts Finder service.

A strict separation of concerns is maintained between this stage and the subsequent data-preparation stage. Phase 1 performs only structural reconciliation — locating each file's true header row, mapping heterogeneous column names onto a common vocabulary, and concatenating the results — and deliberately performs no cleaning: no date parsing, no currency coercion, no de-duplication, no outlier removal. All semantic cleaning remains in Phase 2 (`src/data_engineering/loaders.py` and `clean_merge.py`). This matters for auditability, because it means the consolidated intermediate files are a faithful, inspectable union of the published originals, and any subsequent transformation of a value can be attributed unambiguously to a documented cleaning rule rather than to the acquisition step. Every consolidated row additionally carries `_dataset_source` and `_source_file` provenance columns, so any record in the final analytical panel can be traced back to the specific published monthly file it originated from.

### Documented data-quality problems in the raw corpus

Consolidating six years of independently-produced monthly disclosures surfaced a set of format inconsistencies that are worth reporting in their own right, both because handling them is a substantive part of the methodological contribution and because they are a concrete illustration of the practical barriers to secondary analysis of UK public spending data. The following were identified and addressed.

**Column-name drift across years.** No source maintained a stable schema over the study window. United Lincolnshire Hospitals renamed its spending-category field four times — `Expense Type`, then `Expenditure type` (10 files), then `Nature of spend` (9 files), then `9AN - Level 9 Account Name` (4 files) — renamed its date field from `Date of Payment` to `Date Paid` (25 files) and then `Transaction Date` (1 file), and renamed its monetary field from `AP Amount (£)` to `amount` and then `Analysed Gross` (4 files). NHS England appended a `... for Report` suffix to its expense-type and expense-area headers in its final three monthly files. Casing, trailing whitespace, and currency symbols in header text varied throughout. This was handled by normalising every header to a canonical lower-case alphanumeric form and resolving it through an explicit alias table, so that the mapping from published name to analytical field is declared in one auditable place rather than being implicit in positional assumptions.

**Report-title rows above the true header.** Several exports are spreadsheet reports saved to CSV, and retain a report-title line above the column headings — Bradford's 2023 and 2024 files begin `A3131. Expenditure Over Threshold Report (AP)`, and 17 Lincolnshire files begin `PUBLISHED AREA`. Reading such a file with the default assumption that row 0 is the header produces a frame whose column names are the report title and a series of `Unnamed: n` placeholders; because none of these match the target schema, a subsequent column-alignment step silently yields rows that are structurally present but entirely empty. This failure mode is quiet and destructive, and it accounts for the bulk of the data loss quantified in the next subsection. It was resolved by scanning the first six rows of each file and selecting as the header the first row in which at least three cells resolve to known field aliases.

It is worth correcting a plausible but mistaken characterisation of this problem. These files were initially believed to be *headerless* months whose first row was already data, on the reasoning that their first row does not look like a header. A single-token test for a header is in fact unable to distinguish the two cases in this corpus, because a genuinely headerless Lincolnshire row begins with the literal data value `Department Of Health`, which is textually identical to the corresponding header token. Applying the multi-alias criterion described above to all 216 monthly files established that **no** file in the corpus is genuinely headerless: all 17 apparent cases are title-row files with an intact header on the following line. The distinction is not cosmetic, since the two diagnoses imply different remedies, and the incorrect one silently discards a row from each affected file.

**A tab-separated file with a `.csv` extension.** One Lincolnshire month (`May-19-Published-data.csv`) is tab-delimited despite its extension. Parsed as comma-separated it raises a tokenisation error on its third line, where a quoted thousands separator inside a currency value produces an inconsistent field count. A delimiter-sniffing step was added, comparing tab and comma frequency in each file's opening lines. Without it this month is either lost outright or, worse, admitted as a small number of malformed rows under a spurious column named after the report title — which is precisely what had previously occurred.

**Two incompatible renderings of the same date field.** Bradford published its date column as a plain calendar date (`2019-04-30`) until 2021 and as a zero-time datetime (`2023-06-30 00:00:00`) from 2022 onwards; Lincolnshire mixes a plain date, a fractional-second datetime (`2019-01-10 00:00:00.0`), and UK-format `dd/mm/yyyy` across different months. Both ISO renderings are individually unambiguous, which is what makes the resulting failure so easy to miss: `pandas.to_datetime` infers a single format from the first value it encounters and then coerces every value not matching that format to a null, so concatenating the two halves silently destroys whichever one was not inferred. This affected 7,231 of 13,925 Bradford rows. Because the two renderings differ only in serialisation and not in meaning, the zero time component is stripped during consolidation so that the column has one representation; genuinely ambiguous forms such as `dd/mm/yyyy` are deliberately left untouched, and remain the responsibility of Phase 2's explicit dual-format parser. A small residue of 41 Lincolnshire values are irrecoverably corrupt — they carry a time-only value such as `36:00.0`, apparently the result of a spreadsheet mis-typing the column — and are discarded by Phase 2's completeness filter.

**A month published only in macro-enabled Excel.** Bradford's January 2019 return exists only as `Jan-19.xlsm`; there is no CSV equivalent. Because the acquisition stage prefers CSV where a choice exists and falls back to spreadsheet formats only where it does not, this month is now read (via `openpyxl`) and contributes 101 transactions that were previously absent from the study entirely.

**Non-ASCII and inconsistent filenames, and mixed character encodings.** Filenames contain en-dashes rather than hyphens (for example `Spend-over-25k-–-2020-–-May.csv`), doubled separators, and inconsistent month-name and case conventions, so files cannot be located by pattern-matching on an expected name. File contents are published variously in UTF-8, UTF-8-with-BOM, and Windows-1252; encodings are attempted in sequence so that a single mis-encoded month does not abort the build.

**A schema that drops a field entirely.** Lincolnshire's later exports omit the expense-area dimension altogether, so it is populated for only 3,584 of 8,280 consolidated rows. This is a genuine gap in the published source, not a processing artefact, and it constrains how finely Lincolnshire spending can be decomposed by organisational area.

### The Contracts Finder sector filter: an explicit limitation

The most consequential methodological compromise in this stage concerns Contracts Finder. The export is national and cross-sector: it covers local authorities, education, transport, defence, and central government alongside health. Restricting it to health-sector procurement is therefore unavoidable, but the export contains no authoritative sector classification usable for the purpose — buyer organisations are recorded as free-text names, and the OCDS classification fields are populated too sparsely and too inconsistently across years to serve as a filter.

The approach taken is a keyword filter over the free-text buyer and description fields, matching health-sector terms (`nhs`, `health`, `hospital`, `clinical`, `ambulance`, `blood`, `commissioning support`, `integrated care board`, `hospice`, `primary care`, `care trust`). Applied across the six years it retains 81,482 of 1,035,971 national rows (7.9%): 70,452 of 379,653 notices (18.6%), 628 of 287,062 awards (0.2%), and 10,402 of 369,256 award-supplier links (2.8%).

**This filter is a heuristic and its limitations must be stated plainly, because they bear directly on how the Contracts Finder results may legitimately be interpreted.** Benchmarked against the extract used in the earlier iteration of this project, the filter recovers 39,056 of 39,095 notices (99.90% recall), and 100% of both the award records (250 of 250) and the award-supplier links (2,541 of 2,541). Recall is therefore effectively complete. Precision, however, is deliberately not: measured against that same benchmark, 55.44% of retained notices, 39.81% of retained awards, and 24.43% of retained award-supplier links fall within it. The filter is substantially *broader* than the earlier extract.

Two distinct sources of imprecision should be separated. The first is genuine over-inclusion of health-adjacent public bodies that are not NHS trusts — Public Health England, the Department of Health and Social Care, Health Education England, and the devolved health boards of Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland all match the keyword set. Their inclusion widens the population beyond "NHS England-commissioned procurement" to something closer to "UK public health-sector procurement", which is a defensible unit of analysis but a different one, and it must be described as such. The second is unavoidable lexical false positives: a local-authority notice for "occupational health services" or a university notice mentioning "clinical" research facilities will match on description text alone. No purely lexical filter can exclude these without also excluding legitimate NHS notices whose descriptions are terse, and the design choice made here favours recall over precision on the grounds that a missing notice is invisible and therefore undiagnosable, whereas an over-included one remains inspectable in the provenance columns.

The practical significance of this imprecision is bounded, and the bound is worth making explicit. Contracts Finder records enter the analytical panel as `record_type == "contract_notice"` and are used for contract-level context: notice volumes, award timing, and buyer-supplier network structure. They are **not** used for the study's principal quantitative claims. The Isolation Forest detector, the SHAP attribution, the synthetic-injection benchmark, and the audit red-flag triangulation are all computed exclusively on `record_type == "trust_spend"` records drawn from the three FOI sources, whose sector membership is certain by construction. Any conclusion drawn specifically from the contract-notice subset should nevertheless be read as applying to health-sector public procurement broadly rather than to NHS trust procurement narrowly, and a study whose central question concerned contract notices rather than transactional spending would need a materially stronger sector-classification method than the one used here — most plausibly a curated buyer-organisation reference list rather than free-text keyword matching.

The rebuilt consolidation was validated against the previously-held consolidated files, which were preserved as a comparison baseline before any rebuild took place. The comparison was made on row counts, column presence, per-source-file row counts, aggregate monetary totals, and date ranges.

NHS England reconciles exactly: 271,598 rows from 72 files in both, with byte-identical column sets and an aggregate expenditure total agreeing to the penny (£180,324,729,088.33). This provides a clean positive control, confirming that the consolidation logic reproduces a known-good result where the underlying source is well-behaved.

The other three sources diverge, and in every case the divergence is an improvement whose cause was traced to a specific defect in the earlier processing. Three distinct defects account for the two trust-level gains, and it is worth separating them because they are independent failures that happened to compound.

The first and largest is the title-row failure. In the earlier baseline, 22 Bradford monthly files contributed *zero* usable rows — every one of the 21 files from 2023 and 2024, plus March 2020 — appearing in the consolidated output only as structurally-present but wholly empty records; 7,170 of its 13,864 nominal Bradford rows (51.7%) were empty in this way. The same defect eliminated all 17 title-row Lincolnshire months, one of which (the tab-delimited May 2019 file) contributed 2 malformed rows in place of 91 valid ones.

The second is column-name drift. Lincolnshire's 2024 files use `Date Paid` and `Analysed Gross`, neither of which the earlier alias table recognised, so those months carried null dates and amounts throughout and were discarded wholesale by Phase 2's completeness filter.

The third is the mixed date-rendering problem described above, and it is the reason the coverage windows in the table extend as well as the row counts. Even where rows were structurally recovered, the 7,231 Bradford rows serialised with a zero time component were coerced to null dates and then dropped by the study-window filter — which is why Bradford's baseline coverage appears to stop in February 2022, and Lincolnshire's in July 2021. These were previously documented as gaps in the trusts' *disclosure availability*; they were in fact parsing artefacts. Both trusts publish continuously through December 2024, and both now do so in the panel. This materially changes what the trust-level data can support: the earlier panel had effectively no post-COVID observations for either small trust, making the pre/post comparison for them impossible; the corrected panel covers all three periods for all three trusts.

For NHS England, the alias correction recovered expense-type and expense-area values for 12,833 rows that previously had neither, without altering the row count.

The Contracts Finder increase is of a different character and should not be read as an equivalent correction: it reflects the deliberately broader sector filter documented in the preceding subsection, and is a change in inclusion criteria rather than the repair of a parsing fault.

**Reproducibility note.** The figures in this document are the pipeline's output as run on the author's own machine (Windows 11, Anaconda, Python 3.11, scikit-learn 1.9.0) — the environment used to produce the analysis submitted with this dissertation. Re-running the identical, version-pinned pipeline on a different operating system (a Linux sandbox was used during development for iteration speed) reproduces every rule-based, network-structural, and data-cleaning statistic exactly (e.g. the 326,991-row merged panel, the 57,060 rule-flagged records, the 568 hub suppliers, and all bootstrap/Mann-Whitney/STL results), but shifts the small subset of statistics that depend on the *exact set* of records the Isolation Forest and Local Outlier Factor models flag as anomalous (the triangulation overlap, synthetic F1/PR-AUC, feature-ablation Jaccard, and multi-method consensus count) by one to three percentage points — e.g. the triangulation overlap was 59.65% on Windows versus 57.39% when the identical code and library versions were re-run on Linux. This was investigated directly: forcing single-threaded execution (`n_jobs=1`) instead of the default parallel execution (`n_jobs=-1`) did not change the Linux result, ruling out thread-scheduling non-determinism as the cause. The residual difference is consistent with scikit-learn's own documented position that a fixed `random_state` guarantees reproducibility *within* a given platform and library build, but not bit-for-bit identical floating-point results *across* different operating systems/compiled-library backends, because a handful of procurement records score within floating-point noise of the 98th-percentile cut-off. No qualitative finding changes under either platform's numbers — the overlap remains between roughly 57% and 60% and astronomically non-random either way, and Isolation Forest leads the other three learned detectors under both. This document reports the Windows figures throughout as the single canonical set, since that is the environment in which the final analysis was actually produced.

Four shifts nevertheless merit comment rather than being passed over, since three of them move against the study's own argument and reporting them selectively would be misleading.

The pre-COVID anomaly rate falls from 0.37% to 0.21% while the post-COVID rate rises from 2.64% to 2.79%, so the contrast between periods widens to roughly thirteen-fold. This is the expected consequence of recovering a large volume of trust-level transactions disproportionately drawn from 2023 and 2024: the Isolation Forest is trained on pre-COVID data, and enlarging the later periods relative to the training distribution mechanically increases the share of records lying outside it. The steepening should therefore be interpreted cautiously — it is in part an artefact of the changed period composition rather than purely a substantive finding about procurement behaviour, and the direction of the effect, not its magnitude, is the defensible claim.

The ML/rule triangulation rate falls from 62.7% to 59.65%. This is a modest weakening of one of the study's headline validity arguments and should be reported as such. The likely mechanism is that the recovered records are concentrated in the two small trusts, whose transactions are less likely to satisfy the rule-based red flags — three of the four rules depend on contract-level or COVID-period conditions that trust-level invoice data satisfies less often. The corroboration remains substantial and remains vastly beyond chance (the hypergeometric log-probability of −2,472.7 corresponds to a p-value far below any representable floating-point threshold), but 59.65% is the figure to report.

The Isolation Forest's synthetic-injection F1 falls from 0.315 to 0.2425 and its PR-AUC from 0.182 to 0.1351. Its margin over the three other *learned* detectors remains large — roughly eleven-fold on F1 against the nearest competitor (Local Outlier Factor/One-Class SVM, tied at F1 = 0.0225), comparable in order of magnitude to the original eighteen-fold margin — and the relative ranking among the four detectors, which is what this comparison was designed to establish, is unchanged. This ranking-among-learned-detectors finding should not be read as settling whether Isolation Forest earns its place over a much simpler alternative; the baseline comparison in the Results section below tests that question directly and reaches a less favourable conclusion for the ML layer.

Finally, the hub versus non-hub gap narrows in magnitude (non-hub anomaly rate falling from 3.64% to 2.89%, hub from 1.16% to 1.02%, with the hub-supplier count growing from n=315 to n=340) while remaining highly statistically significant (p = 1.26 × 10⁻¹⁸ versus 5.4 × 10⁻²² previously), the latter reflecting the larger supplier sample. The direction of the finding — the one most emphasised in the discussion below — is unaffected.

A residual limitation of this validation should be acknowledged. The comparison baseline is the project's own earlier consolidation, not an independently-produced authoritative dataset, so it establishes that the rebuild is more complete than the earlier attempt and that the discrepancies have identified causes — but it cannot establish absolute correctness against the publishing bodies' own totals. Verifying the consolidated figures against each trust's published annual accounts would be a stronger validation and is a reasonable direction for further work.

## Methodology — Advanced Validation and Robustness Methods

The core anomaly-detection pipeline (Isolation Forest trained on pre-COVID data, scored across all periods, explained with SHAP, and validated against literature-derived audit red flags) leaves four open questions that a single-model, single-metric design cannot answer on its own: (1) is the anomaly signal specific to Isolation Forest, or would other unsupervised detectors agree; (2) in the absence of ground-truth fraud labels, how sensitive is the detector to anomalies of a *known* magnitude and type; (3) are the reported differences between COVID periods statistically distinguishable from noise, or could they be produced by resampling variation in a panel of this size; and (4) does the anomaly signal cluster around structurally important ("hub") suppliers, as procurement-collusion literature would predict, or elsewhere in the supplier network. Four additional analysis stages were added to address each question directly.

### Multi-method anomaly detector comparison

Isolation Forest (Liu, Ting & Zhou, 2008) is an ensemble method that isolates anomalies via random recursive partitioning, exploiting the fact that anomalous points require fewer splits to isolate than points in dense regions. To test whether the pipeline's findings are an artefact of this specific inductive bias, three alternative unsupervised anomaly detectors were trained on the identical pre-COVID feature set (log-amount, within-category amount z-score, days since the supplier's last transaction, transaction sequence number, new-supplier flag, month, day-of-week) and used to score the same held-out records:

- **Local Outlier Factor** (LOF; Breunig et al., 2000) — a density-based method that flags points whose local density is substantially lower than that of their k-nearest neighbours.
- **One-Class SVM** (Schölkopf et al., 2001) — a boundary-based method that learns a decision function separating the bulk of the training distribution from the origin in a kernel-induced feature space, trained on an 8,000-record subsample of the pre-COVID data for computational tractability.
- **MLP-Autoencoder** — a reconstruction-based neural method: a multi-layer perceptron is trained to reconstruct its own input from a compressed bottleneck representation, and anomaly score is defined as reconstruction error; points the network cannot reconstruct well are flagged (98th-percentile reconstruction-error threshold).

All four methods were calibrated to flag approximately 2% of records, matching the Isolation Forest threshold used elsewhere in the pipeline, so that flag-rate differences cannot explain any observed disagreement. Agreement between each pair of methods was quantified using the Jaccard index, \( J(A,B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} \), applied to the sets of flagged record indices from each method. A consensus anomaly indicator (\( \geq 2 \) of 4 methods agreeing) was also constructed as a more conservative, cross-validated anomaly signal for sensitivity analysis. Implementation: `src/modeling/method_comparison.py`.

### Synthetic anomaly injection evaluation

Public procurement datasets of this kind do not carry ground-truth fraud/error labels, which is the central methodological limitation of any unsupervised anomaly-detection evaluation (a limitation explicitly acknowledged in Section 5, Validation). Following the simulation-based evaluation design used by Agyemang (2024) to benchmark unsupervised anomaly detectors on data without ground-truth labels, a synthetic-injection benchmark was constructed: 400 synthetic anomalies (2% of a 20,000-record held-out sample) were generated across three archetypes chosen to mimic plausible procurement-fraud patterns from the NHS Counter Fraud Authority's published risk indicators (NHS Counter Fraud Authority, 2022):

1. **Invoice inflation** — an existing transaction's amount is multiplied by a randomly drawn factor in the range 5×–15×, simulating price manipulation on an otherwise legitimate order.
2. **Ghost-vendor burst** — a transaction is rewritten to simulate a brand-new supplier's first-ever, unusually large transaction (new-supplier flag set, transaction sequence reset to 1, recency reset to 0 days, amount inflated relative to the category's 90th percentile), simulating shell-company or emergency direct-award risk.
3. **Round-number kickback** — a transaction's amount is replaced with an exact multiple of a "round" magnitude (£10,000 / £25,000 / £50,000 / £100,000 / £250,000), simulating invoice manipulation via suspiciously round settlement amounts.

Because the identity and count of injected anomalies is known by construction, precision, recall, F1, and precision-recall AUC (PR-AUC) could be computed exactly for each of the four detectors described above, both in aggregate and broken down by injection type. Implementation: `src/modeling/synthetic_evaluation.py`.

### Statistical significance testing

Three complementary statistical tests were applied to establish whether the pipeline's headline findings are distinguishable from sampling noise, addressing a gap in the original design (which reported point estimates only):

- **Exact hypergeometric test** on the ML/rule-based triangulation overlap (Section 5). Given a population of \(N\) scored records, \(K\) of which are rule-flagged and \(n\) of which are ML-flagged, the hypergeometric distribution gives the exact probability of observing at least the actual overlap \(k\) by chance alone if ML flags were assigned independently of rule flags: \( P(X \geq k) = \sum_{i=k}^{\min(n,K)} \frac{\binom{K}{i}\binom{N-K}{n-i}}{\binom{N}{n}} \). This replaces a permutation-test approach with an exact closed-form test appropriate for this kind of overlap-count problem.
- **Bootstrap confidence intervals** (2,000 resamples with replacement) around the Isolation Forest anomaly rate, new-supplier rate, and STL percentage deviation from baseline, computed separately for each COVID period, to quantify estimation uncertainty around each point estimate given finite sample sizes per period.
- **Mann-Whitney U test** on the full (unthresholded) Isolation Forest anomaly-score distributions between each pair of periods, with the rank-biserial correlation reported as a non-parametric effect size. This tests whether the entire score distribution — not just the small flagged tail — shifted between periods, which is a stronger and more sample-efficient test than comparing flag rates alone.

Implementation: `src/analysis/statistical_tests.py`.

### Supplier-buyer network analysis

Procurement-fraud literature identifies supplier network position as a potential collusion indicator: entities embedded across multiple buyers or occupying structurally central positions may be better placed to coordinate bid-rigging or extract favourable, repeated awards (Victor et al., 2024; Lyra, 2024). To test this hypothesis empirically, a bipartite graph was constructed with buyer trusts and suppliers as the two node types, and an edge between a buyer and supplier if they transacted at least twice (`NETWORK_MIN_TRANSACTIONS = 2`, to exclude one-off relationships from centrality calculations), following the two-mode/bipartite network methodology of Borgatti & Everett (1997). Degree and betweenness centrality (Freeman, 1977) were computed on this graph, and "hub" suppliers were defined as the union of (a) multi-trust suppliers (degree ≥ 2 on the buyer side) and (b) high-volume suppliers (top 5% by total transaction count). Mean Isolation Forest anomaly rate was then compared between hub and non-hub suppliers using a Mann-Whitney U test.

A second, same-side co-occurrence projection graph was built by linking pairs of suppliers whenever they appeared together within the same buyer + spending-category + calendar-month group (capped at a maximum group size of 40 to bound computational cost on large groups), producing a weighted graph where edge weight reflects the number of shared co-occurrence groups. Communities were detected using greedy modularity maximisation (Clauset, Newman & Moore, 2004), and mean anomaly rate was computed per community to test whether anomalies concentrate in particular supplier sub-groups rather than being uniformly distributed. Implementation: `src/network/supplier_network.py`.

## Results — Advanced Validation and Robustness Methods

### Multi-method comparison

Figure X (`method_agreement_heatmap.png`) shows pairwise Jaccard agreement between the four detectors on pre-COVID-trained models (`data/processed/method_comparison_summary_agreement.csv`). Isolation Forest disagrees sharply with all three alternatives (Jaccard 0.071–0.093: 0.093 vs. Local Outlier Factor, 0.092 vs. One-Class SVM, 0.071 vs. the Autoencoder), while Local Outlier Factor, One-Class SVM, and the MLP-Autoencoder cluster together (pairwise Jaccard 0.42–0.67). This is a substantively important finding: it indicates Isolation Forest is not simply a noisier or better-calibrated version of the same "outlierness" concept as the other three; it is capturing a qualitatively different structural property of the data — records that are easy to isolate via a small number of feature-value combinations — whereas the density/boundary/reconstruction-based trio are converging on records that are unusual relative to the *bulk* of the training distribution in a more holistic sense. A consensus flag (≥2 of 4 methods agreeing) identified 5,751 of 271,343 scored records (2.12%) as anomalous by more than one independent detection paradigm, providing a more conservative anomaly set for readers who want cross-method corroboration in addition to the rule-based triangulation already reported in Section 5.

### Synthetic anomaly injection evaluation

Figure X (`synthetic_precision_recall.png`) reports precision, recall, and F1 for all methods on the 400-anomaly synthetic benchmark (`data/processed/synthetic_injection_evaluation.csv`, `..._by_type.csv`). Of the four *learned* detectors, Isolation Forest is the strongest: F1 = 0.2425 versus 0.0225 (LOF and One-Class SVM, tied) and 0.0200 (MLP-Autoencoder); PR-AUC = 0.1351 versus 0.0315–0.0572 for the alternatives. Breaking this down by injection type: for the ghost-vendor-burst archetype (jointly extreme on new-supplier flag, transaction recency, and amount), Isolation Forest recalls 54.5% of injected cases compared to 0% for LOF, One-Class SVM, and the Autoencoder. For invoice-inflation, Isolation Forest recalls 12.5% versus 3.4–3.7% for the alternatives, and for round-number-kickback, 5.4% versus 2.3–3.1%. This pattern is consistent with Isolation Forest's partitioning mechanism being particularly well-suited to detecting anomalies defined by a *joint* combination of several extreme feature values (as in the ghost-vendor-burst case) relative to anomalies defined by a single extreme feature (amount alone), where density- and boundary-based methods can partially compensate. Isolation Forest is therefore the best-performing of the four *unsupervised, general-purpose* detectors — but see the baseline comparison immediately below, which is a materially more demanding bar.

### Baseline comparison: does the ML layer beat a one-line rule?

The preceding comparison only asks whether Isolation Forest beats other *learned* detectors. A sharper and more important question, raised directly in supervisor feedback, is whether it beats the trivial, non-learned heuristic that a human auditor would reach for first: flag a transaction if its supplier is new **and** its amount sits in the top 2% for its spending category. This heuristic is exactly rule R3 (`flag_new_supplier_large_covid`, restated without the COVID-period restriction) expressed as a continuous, rankable score (`fit_new_supplier_amount_baseline` in `src/modeling/synthetic_evaluation.py`) so it can be benchmarked on identical footing — same 400-anomaly synthetic sample, same 98th-percentile flagging threshold, same precision/recall/F1/PR-AUC metrics — as the four learned detectors above.

The result does not favour the ML layer. The baseline achieves F1 = 0.3525 and PR-AUC = 0.3318, versus Isolation Forest's F1 = 0.2425 and PR-AUC = 0.1351 — the trivial rule outperforms the full unsupervised model on this benchmark by a wide margin. Breaking this down by injection type shows exactly why: the baseline recalls 100% of ghost-vendor-burst injections (versus Isolation Forest's 54.5%), because that injection type is constructed to set `is_new_supplier=1` and inflate the amount 1.5–3× above the category's 90th percentile (`_inject_ghost_vendor_burst`) — precisely the two conditions the baseline rule checks for directly, whereas Isolation Forest must infer the same joint condition indirectly through a learned partitioning of seven features. On the other two injection types, which do not manipulate `is_new_supplier`, the baseline is weaker than Isolation Forest (invoice-inflation: 1.5% vs. 12.5%; round-number-kickback: 3.9% vs. 5.4%), since a large share of those injected anomalies are not even new-supplier transactions and so cannot be flagged by the baseline at all.

The honest conclusion is that Isolation Forest's aggregate advantage over the *learned* alternatives (LOF, One-Class SVM, Autoencoder) does not extend to the *naive* alternative, on this synthetic benchmark. Isolation Forest is not simply reproducing the baseline rule — it recalls meaningfully on injection types the baseline structurally cannot detect at all — but on the specific onboarding-risk pattern (new supplier + large amount) that both the SHAP analysis (Section 4) and this project's central narrative treat as the dominant anomaly archetype, a one-line rule matches or beats the seven-feature unsupervised model. This should be read as a real constraint on the claimed value-add of the ML layer, not explained away: for the single most emphasised anomaly pattern in this dissertation, the case for a full Isolation Forest model over a simple audit rule is not established by this benchmark, and the more defensible framing is that Isolation Forest adds value primarily by generalising to anomaly patterns a fixed rule set does not anticipate (as the invoice-inflation and round-number-kickback results show), rather than by being a superior detector of the new-supplier-plus-large-amount pattern specifically. A synthetic benchmark of only 400 injected cases in three fixed archetypes is itself a limited test of this claim and should not be over-generalised, but on the evidence available, the answer to "does Isolation Forest clearly beat a one-line rule?" is no for this specific pattern, and this document reports that plainly rather than presenting the ML layer as an unqualified improvement.

### Statistical significance testing

The hypergeometric exact test on the ML/rule-based triangulation overlap (N = 326,991 scored records, K = 57,060 rule-flagged, n = 5,427 ML-flagged, observed overlap = 3,237 against an expected-by-chance overlap of 947.0, i.e. 59.65% of ML flags corroborated versus a 17.5% chance rate), returned a log-probability of approximately −2,472.7 (i.e. the exact p-value underflows standard floating-point representation to zero). This confirms the ML/rule corroboration is not attributable to chance overlap between two independently distributed flag sets, and provides a rigorous statistical basis — beyond the descriptive percentage alone — for treating the two detection approaches as measuring a shared underlying signal.

Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (2,000 resamples) show non-overlapping ranges for the Isolation Forest anomaly rate across periods — pre-COVID 0.205% [0.165%, 0.250%], COVID 1.577% [1.494%, 1.662%], post-COVID 2.786% [2.698%, 2.869%] — confirming the period-over-period increase reported in Section 3 is not an artefact of sampling noise given the finite (though large) number of records per period. The equivalent intervals for the new-supplier rate are pre-COVID 1.215% [1.112%, 1.315%], COVID 3.437% [3.317%, 3.562%], post-COVID 1.844% [1.775%, 1.911%] — again non-overlapping. The STL percentage-deviation intervals are wider (pre-COVID 0.02% [−5.23%, 5.36%], n=15 months; COVID +33.43% [23.79%, 42.91%], n=23 months; post-COVID +40.54% [29.86%, 53.74%], n=34 months) because the effective sample size is the number of *months* in each period, not the number of transactions; these are reported as indicative given the acknowledged autocorrelation in monthly time-series data (see Limitations).

The Mann-Whitney U test on full anomaly-score distributions (not just the flagged tail) was significant at p ≈ 0 for all three period-pair comparisons, with rank-biserial effect sizes of 0.4462 (pre-COVID vs. COVID), 0.6168 (pre-COVID vs. post-COVID), and 0.2284 (COVID vs. post-COVID). The largest effect size being pre-COVID vs. post-COVID — larger than pre-COVID vs. COVID itself — is a notable finding: it indicates the *overall* anomaly-score distribution continued to diverge from the pre-pandemic baseline even after the acute COVID period ended, corroborating the STL and Isolation-Forest-rate findings that procurement behaviour did not revert to its pre-pandemic norm.

### Triangulation circularity check

A legitimate methodological objection to the 59.65% ML/rule overlap above is that it may be partly circular rather than fully independent corroboration. Rule R3 (`flag_new_supplier_large_covid`) and the Isolation Forest feature set both key on the same underlying signal: `is_new_supplier` is an Isolation Forest input feature directly, and `supplier_txn_seq` — the SHAP-dominant feature in 179/200 top anomalies (Section 4) — is highly correlated with it, since a low transaction-sequence number is close to definitionally what "new" means. If R3 is doing most of the corroborating work, the headline overlap would overstate how independent the two detection approaches really are.

This is tested directly by re-running the hypergeometric triangulation test with R3 excluded, so that only the three rules with no shared input (`flag_direct_award_covid`, `flag_price_spike`, `flag_round_amount`) are used to define "rule-flagged" (`hypergeometric_triangulation_circularity_check()` in `src/analysis/statistical_tests.py`; see `data/processed/statistical_tests_summary_hypergeometric_circularity_check.csv`). The result: excluding R3 changes the overlap from 3,237/5,427 (59.65%) to 3,232/5,427 (59.55%) — a difference of 5 records — with the hypergeometric significance essentially unchanged (log(p) = −2,463.1 versus −2,472.7). The overlap is not built on R3: removing the one rule that shares an input with the ML model barely moves the corroboration rate at all, because the large majority of the overlap is being carried by the three feature-independent rules (direct award, price spike, round amount). This is a reassuring result for the study's triangulation argument — the corroboration is not a circular artefact of a shared feature, though the caveat that `is_new_supplier`/`supplier_txn_seq` still drive the *Isolation Forest* side of the comparison (independently of which rules are compared against it) remains worth stating for completeness.

### Supplier-buyer network analysis

The bipartite buyer-supplier graph comprised 8 buyer trusts, 5,597 suppliers, and 6,122 edges meeting the ≥2-transaction threshold (5,602 nodes total). Applying the hub definition (multi-trust suppliers, n=375, union high-volume suppliers, top 5% by transaction count, threshold 197 transactions, n=280) yielded 568 hub suppliers. Contrary to the collusion-network hypothesis that motivated this analysis, Figure X (`network_hub_comparison.png`) shows hub suppliers have a *markedly lower* mean anomaly rate (1.023%, n=340 scored hub suppliers) than non-hub suppliers (2.886%, n=3,556), a difference confirmed by Mann-Whitney U test (p = 1.26 × 10⁻¹⁸). This is an important and somewhat counter-intuitive empirical result worth foregrounding rather than downplaying: it suggests that, in this dataset, anomalous transactions are concentrated among smaller, newer, single-relationship suppliers rather than large, well-established, multi-trust incumbents. This is directionally consistent with the new-supplier-rate and SHAP findings elsewhere in the pipeline (Sections 2 and 4), which already identify early-relationship transactions as the dominant anomaly driver, and suggests the anomaly signal detected here is better characterised as "onboarding-risk" than "incumbent-collusion" risk — a meaningful refinement of the original research question's framing that should be discussed explicitly in the dissertation's discussion/conclusion chapter.

The co-occurrence projection graph (2,636 suppliers, 54,567 edges, derived from 2,896 eligible buyer+category+month pools) was partitioned into 32 communities via greedy modularity maximisation (modularity Q = 0.6500, indicating a strong community structure). Figure X (`community_anomaly_rate.png`) shows the communities (size ≥ 3) ranked by mean Isolation Forest anomaly rate: community 10 (n=9) had the highest mean anomaly rate at 3.704%, followed by community 7 (n=59, 3.427%), community 2 (n=82, 3.318%), community 6 (n=70, 2.895%), and community 4 (n=230, 2.757%) — all above the overall 2.00% baseline — while community 5 (n=36) sat lowest at 0.625% and community 14 (n=3) recorded 0.000%, a greater than 5-fold spread across communities with ≥3 members. This indicates that while hub *position* (degree/betweenness) is inversely associated with anomaly rate, certain buyer/category/month supplier *pools* nonetheless carry disproportionate anomaly risk, which may be a more actionable unit of audit prioritisation than individual supplier centrality alone.

## Suggested discussion points for the dissertation's Discussion/Conclusion chapter

- The multi-method comparison substantiates Isolation Forest as the best choice *among the four learned detectors*: it is measurably better suited to detecting the joint-feature-extreme anomalies (new supplier + no history + large amount) that dominate this domain's risk profile, at some cost in sensitivity to single-feature anomalies (pure amount inflation) that the other methods partially compensate for. However, the baseline comparison (Results section) shows this advantage does not extend to a trivial "new supplier + top-2% amount" rule, which beats Isolation Forest outright on the synthetic benchmark's dominant anomaly archetype. The defensible claim is narrower than "Isolation Forest is the right tool for this problem": it is the best of the four learned detectors tested, and it generalises to anomaly patterns a fixed rule set does not anticipate, but for the specific pattern this dissertation emphasises most, a human-written rule is not clearly beaten by the ML layer. A production system might reasonably run the simple rule as a cheap first pass and reserve Isolation Forest for catching the anomaly types the rule structurally misses, rather than treating the ML model as a strict upgrade over rule-based screening.
- The statistical testing results substantially strengthen the evidentiary basis of the pipeline's core claims (period-over-period increases in anomaly and new-supplier rates, and the ML/rule triangulation), moving them from descriptive point estimates to statistically corroborated findings — directly addressing a "how do you know this isn't just noise?" objection a marker would reasonably raise.
- The network analysis's counter-intuitive hub finding is arguably the most original empirical contribution of the enhanced pipeline: it reframes the practical implication of the anomaly-detection results away from "watch large incumbent suppliers for collusion" and toward "prioritise onboarding controls and early-relationship due diligence for new/low-volume suppliers," which is both a more specific and a more actionable recommendation than the original research question anticipated.
- All four enhancements should be explicitly framed as *robustness and validity* work rather than *headline result* work — they interrogate whether the original Isolation Forest + SHAP + rule-triangulation findings hold up under alternative methods, synthetic ground truth, formal significance testing, and an alternative theoretical lens (network position), and in every case they corroborate or meaningfully refine rather than contradict the original findings.

## Limitations of the advanced methods (for the dissertation's Limitations section)

- The synthetic injection benchmark evaluates detector sensitivity against three specific, hand-designed anomaly archetypes chosen to reflect published fraud-risk indicators; it demonstrates *relative* sensitivity to those archetypes and should not be read as a general-purpose accuracy estimate against all possible real-world fraud/error patterns.
- The hub/non-hub network finding is descriptive and associational, not causal. A lower observed anomaly rate among hub suppliers could reflect genuinely lower-risk incumbent behaviour, or could partly reflect the underlying Isolation Forest feature set (transaction recency, new-supplier flag, transaction sequence number) being structurally biased toward flagging early-relationship transactions regardless of supplier network position. This should be flagged as a direction for further work rather than presented as definitive evidence against a collusion hypothesis.
- Bootstrap confidence intervals on the STL percentage-deviation estimates are indicative rather than exact, because the naive i.i.d. bootstrap assumes independent observations, while monthly time-series values are autocorrelated; a block-bootstrap approach would be a more rigorous extension.
- The One-Class SVM was trained on an 8,000-record subsample of the pre-COVID training data (rather than the full ~44,167-record set) for computational tractability within the sandboxed environment used for this coursework; this is a reasonable approximation given One-Class SVM's super-linear training complexity, but means its comparison results carry slightly more sampling variance than the other three methods.

## Methodology — Phase 7: Composite Risk Scoring, Category Deep Dive, Robustness Checks, and BI Delivery

The Phase 6 additions established that the anomaly signal is method-robust, statistically significant, sensitive to synthetically injected fraud patterns, and inversely associated with supplier network centrality. Phase 7 extends the project from *detection and validation* to *actionable delivery*: which individual suppliers and spend categories should an auditor look at first, how sensitive are the headline findings to modelling choices a marker might reasonably question, and how can the results be handed to a non-technical stakeholder in a business-intelligence format they can explore themselves.

### Phase 7A — Composite supplier risk score

Individual anomaly-detection outputs (Isolation Forest flag, audit red-flag rule, network hub status) each capture a different facet of supplier risk but none alone is a complete risk ranking. Following the "composite risk index" approach used in the public-procurement corruption-risk literature — Fazekas, Tóth & King (2016), *An Objective Corruption Risk Index Using Public Procurement Data*, *European Journal on Criminal Policy and Research*, 22(3), 369–397, and Abdou, Basdevant, Dávid-Barrett & Fazekas (2022), *Assessing Vulnerabilities to Corruption in Public Procurement and Their Price Impact*, IMF Working Paper No. 2022/094 — three independent per-supplier signals are percentile-ranked (0–100) and blended with equal weights into a single composite score:

1. **Anomaly rate** — share of the supplier's own transactions flagged by the Phase 4 Isolation Forest.
2. **Mean anomaly score** — the supplier's average *continuous* anomaly score, capturing magnitude (a supplier barely over the 98th-percentile cut and one far beyond it both score identically on signal 1 alone).
3. **Rule-flag rate** — share of transactions matching ≥1 literature-derived audit red flag (Phase 5).

Percentile-ranking each signal before blending is a standard composite-indicator normalisation step (OECD/JRC *Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators*, 2008), needed because the three raw signals live on very different natural scales. Network hub status (Phase 6D) is deliberately **excluded** from the score itself — since hub suppliers were already found to have a *lower* raw anomaly rate, folding centrality into the composite score would bias it in the wrong direction — and is instead retained only as a descriptive column for cross-referencing. Suppliers with fewer than `config.SUPPLIER_RISK_MIN_TRANSACTIONS` transactions are excluded from ranking, since a single anomalous transaction out of one or two total is too thin a basis for a supplier-level judgement. Risk tiers (Low/Medium/High/Critical) are assigned from empirical percentile cut-points of the composite score. Implementation: `src/analysis/supplier_risk_score.py`.

### Phase 7B — Category-level deep dive

The headline STL/HHI/anomaly results (Phases 3–4) are reported at the whole-panel level, which conceals which specific spend categories actually drove the aggregate COVID-19 shock. This module disaggregates every metric (total spend, anomaly rate, new-supplier rate, HHI, share of period spend) by `category × period`, and separately ranks categories by pre-COVID → COVID spend growth, directly testing the a-priori expectation — drawn from the National Audit Office's *Investigation into government procurement during the COVID-19 pandemic* (HC 959, Session 2019–2021, 26 November 2020), which found PPE accounted for 80% of pandemic-contract volume and 68% of contract value — that medical/PPE-adjacent categories would show the sharpest COVID-period spend growth and supplier churn. Categories are only ranked by growth if they have ≥20 transactions total, to avoid a handful of pre-COVID pounds producing an uninterpretably large percentage swing. HHI within each category × period cell uses the same definition as the project-wide Phase 3 HHI (sum of squared supplier spend shares × 10,000; Rhoades, 1993, *The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index*, *Federal Reserve Bulletin*, 79, 188–189). Implementation: `src/analysis/category_deep_dive.py`.

### Phase 7C — Robustness checks

Every headline result up to Phase 6 rests on three modelling choices that are each, to some degree, a judgement call: the 98th-percentile anomaly-score cut-off, the exact calendar dates used to split pre/COVID/post periods, and the feature set fed to Isolation Forest. Reporting sensitivity of headline findings to reasonable variation in each is standard practice for unsupervised anomaly-detection studies that lack ground-truth labels (Aggarwal, 2017, *Outlier Analysis*, 2nd ed., Springer, ch. 12; Emmott, Das, Dietterich, Fern & Wong, 2015, *A Meta-Analysis of the Anomaly Detection Problem*, arXiv:1503.01158). Three checks were run, each re-using artefacts already produced earlier in the pipeline rather than duplicating expensive model retraining where avoidable:

- **(a) Threshold sensitivity** — re-flag anomalies at the 95th/98th/99th percentile of the *same* already-computed anomaly score, and recompute the period-level anomaly rate and ML/rule triangulation hypergeometric test at each cut-off.
- **(b) Period-boundary sensitivity** — shift the COVID start/end boundaries ±1 calendar month (applied symmetrically) and recompute period-level anomaly rates under each shifted definition, using the same per-record scores.
- **(c) Feature-set ablation** — retrain Isolation Forest with `is_new_supplier` (the single most policy-salient engineered feature) removed, and compare the resulting flags against the full-feature baseline via Jaccard index (flag-set overlap) and Spearman rank correlation (continuous-score agreement).

Implementation: `src/analysis/robustness_checks.py`.

### Phase 7D/7E — BI-ready data export and interactive dashboard

To make the pipeline's outputs usable by a non-technical stakeholder without requiring them to run Python, all Phase 2–7C outputs were consolidated into a clean, denormalised **star schema** (one fact table of 326,991 transactions plus four dimension tables — supplier, category, period, and month) and exported as plain CSVs (`src/analysis/bi_export.py`), together with a self-contained interactive Plotly dashboard mirroring a Tableau/Power BI-style multi-panel layout (`src/analysis/dashboard.py`).

**A note on the deliverable format, in the interest of transparency.** A genuine Power BI (`.pbix`) file is a proprietary binary format that no Python library, open-source tool, or LLM-authorable process can reliably produce — only Power BI Desktop itself can write one. A Tableau (`.twbx`) workbook is technically a zip of XML plus a data extract and is *theoretically* hand-constructible, but doing so without Tableau installed to open and validate the result is unverifiable and likely to yield a file that silently fails to open. Rather than risk submitting a fabricated binary that cannot be checked, this project instead ships the CSV star-schema export plus a working interactive HTML dashboard (viewable in any browser, no BI software required) plus a build guide (`docs/bi_dashboard_guide.md`) that maps every dashboard panel to the exact fields and chart types needed to reconstruct it in Tableau Desktop or Power BI Desktop in a few minutes. This is a deliberate, disclosed trade-off, not a silent substitution.

## Results — Phase 7: Composite Risk Scoring, Category Deep Dive, Robustness Checks

### Composite supplier risk score

Of 7,713 total distinct suppliers, 3,682 had ≥`SUPPLIER_RISK_MIN_TRANSACTIONS` transactions and were scored. The resulting risk-tier distribution is Low = 2,209, Medium = 920, High = 368, Critical = 185. Figure X (`supplier_risk_score.png`) visualises the tier distribution and the top-ranked suppliers. Comparing hub vs. non-hub suppliers on the *composite* score (rather than the raw anomaly rate examined in Phase 6D) shows hub suppliers actually score **higher** on average (64.08, n=334) than non-hub suppliers (48.61, n=3,348), a difference confirmed by Mann-Whitney U test (p = 3.64 × 10⁻⁵⁶). This is the opposite direction from the Phase 6D raw-anomaly-rate finding and is worth flagging explicitly rather than glossing over: it arises because the composite score also incorporates rule-flag rate and mean anomaly-score *magnitude*, not just the binary anomaly flag rate that Phase 6D examined, and high-volume hub suppliers accumulate more total transactions in which at least one audit red flag can fire even while their *per-transaction* anomaly rate stays comparatively low. The two findings are not contradictory but measure genuinely different things — raw per-transaction anomaly propensity (Phase 6D, where hubs look safer) versus accumulated audit-flag exposure across a supplier's full transaction history (Phase 7A, where hubs look riskier because they simply transact far more) — and this nuance should be discussed explicitly in the dissertation's discussion chapter as a reason composite indices need careful specification, not treated as a contradiction to be hidden.

### Interpreting the composite risk score: implications for procurement strategy

A composite score is only useful to a dissertation reader, and to the procurement function it is ultimately intended to serve, if its numerical output is explicitly connected back to a decision. This subsection makes that connection, since a risk score with no stated consequence is not yet a contribution to practice.

The four risk tiers reported above map naturally onto a graduated response, consistent with a risk-based (rather than uniform) approach to procurement assurance, which is the approach the NHS Counter Fraud Authority's own guidance recommends over blanket auditing of all spend (NHS Counter Fraud Authority, 2022): **Critical**-tier suppliers (185) warrant a manual case review before their next payment or contract renewal, escalated to a counter-fraud or internal-audit function rather than resolved at the transactional level; **High**-tier suppliers (368) warrant a second-approver control and supporting documentation before the relationship is extended further; **Medium**-tier suppliers (920) warrant inclusion in the next periodic (rather than immediate) spend review; and **Low**-tier suppliers (2,209) require no departure from standard controls. This graduated structure is itself a substantive output of the analysis, not an afterthought: it converts a continuous 0–100 score, which is not directly actionable, into a small number of discrete operational responses that a non-technical reviewer can apply without needing to interpret the underlying model.

The more consequential interpretive point is *which* transactions drive the score upward, because it determines where a procurement function should concentrate limited assurance effort. The SHAP results (Section "Explainability", `supplier_txn_seq` dominant in 179/200 top anomalies) and the network-hub finding (hub suppliers 1.02% anomaly rate vs. 2.89% for non-hub, Section "Supplier-buyer network analysis") point in the same direction and should be read together: the anomaly signal in this dataset concentrates in the *early transactions of new supplier relationships*, not in the size of a payment or the scale of an established supplier. This is a materially different, and more specific, practical implication than the intuitive assumption a reader might otherwise bring to a procurement-fraud study — that oversight effort should be weighted toward the largest contracts or the most prominent incumbent suppliers. The evidence here instead supports weighting oversight toward supplier *onboarding*: the first several transactions with any new supplier, irrespective of that supplier's eventual size. The category-level finding (below) provides a complementary, orthogonal prioritisation axis — specific spend categories, rather than specific suppliers, that grew disproportionately during the COVID period — and a mature assurance programme would reasonably combine both axes (new-supplier transactions, within the highest-growth categories) rather than treating them as competing recommendations.

It should be stated plainly that this is a prioritisation framework, not a fraud-determination tool. A Critical-tier score means a supplier's transaction pattern deviates from pre-pandemic norms more than the population's 98th–99th percentile and is independently corroborated by a literature-derived audit heuristic in a majority of cases (Section 5's 59.65% overlap) — it is evidence that review effort spent there is more likely to be productive than review effort spent elsewhere, not a finding that fraud or error has occurred. This distinction between *statistical prioritisation* and *confirmed audit finding* should be preserved throughout any discussion of the composite score's practical use, both in this dissertation and in any operational deployment a reader might consider.

### Category-level deep dive

Ranking categories by pre-COVID → COVID spend growth (categories with ≥20 transactions only) surfaces "CompSwrPrch Additions" as the largest percentage grower (+2,259.8%, from £124,429 to £2.94 million), followed by "Med & Surg Equip General" (+2,054.8%, from £308,125 to £6.64 million), "MSSE Consumables" (+1,288.7%, from £197,929 to £2.75 million), "Diagnostic Tests Non NHS" (+1,206.5%, from £107,255 to £1.40 million), and "CNST — NHS Resolution" (+993.3%, from £2.90 million to £31.65 million). Unlike an earlier iteration of this ranking, none of the current top five categories has a pre-COVID base spend below £100,000, so none of these percentage swings is a small-base artefact in the way flagged as a risk in the methodology above — each combines a substantial percentage increase with a genuine multi-million-pound (or, for CompSwrPrch Additions, multi-hundred-thousand-pound) absolute increase. "Med & Surg Equip General" and "CNST — NHS Resolution" show the largest absolute-pound increases and are consistent a priori with the PPE/medical-equipment procurement surge documented in the NAO's HC 959 investigation. Figure X (`category_covid_shock.png`) shows the top categories by growth with both absolute and percentage framing to make this distinction visible.

### Robustness checks

**Threshold sensitivity**: the period ordering (pre-COVID < COVID < post-COVID anomaly rate) is stable across all three thresholds tested — at the 95th percentile, rates are 0.958% / 4.818% / 6.344%; at 98th (baseline), 0.205% / 1.577% / 2.786%; at 99th, 0.056% / 0.600% / 1.512%. The ML/rule overlap rate *increases* as the threshold tightens (42.68% at 95th → 59.65% at 98th → 75.76% at 99th), indicating that the most extreme anomalies by Isolation Forest score are progressively more likely to be independently corroborated by a rule-based red flag — a reassuring robustness property, since it means the model's highest-confidence flags are also its most externally validated ones.

**Period-boundary sensitivity**: shifting the COVID start/end boundaries by ±1 calendar month leaves the qualitative period ordering unchanged (pre-COVID rate 0.21–0.26% across all three boundary definitions; COVID rate 1.35–1.88%; post-COVID rate 2.67–2.84%), confirming the headline finding is not an artefact of the exact boundary dates chosen.

**Feature-set ablation**: removing `is_new_supplier` from the Isolation Forest feature set and retraining yields a Jaccard overlap of 0.707 between the ablated and full-feature flag sets, and a Spearman rank correlation of 0.925 (p ≈ 0) between the two continuous anomaly-score vectors (`data/processed/robustness_feature_ablation.csv`). This indicates the model's overall ranking of "how anomalous" a record is remains highly stable without this one feature, though roughly 29% of specific flagged records do change — showing `is_new_supplier` meaningfully contributes to *which* records cross the flagging threshold at the margin, even though it is not solely responsible for the model's overall discriminative signal.

Implementation and full sensitivity tables: `src/analysis/robustness_checks.py`, `data/processed/robustness_*.csv`.

## Additional discussion points for the dissertation's Discussion/Conclusion chapter (Phase 7)

- The divergent hub-supplier finding between Phase 6D (raw anomaly rate: hubs lower-risk) and Phase 7A (composite risk score: hubs higher-risk) is itself a useful methodological lesson worth discussing: a single summary statistic (raw flag rate) and a multi-signal composite index can legitimately point in different directions when they aggregate different underlying quantities, and a dissertation reader should be shown this explicitly rather than have one number silently override the other.
- The category deep dive's "Computer Hardware Purch" small-base artefact is a good worked example, in the dissertation's methodology or limitations section, of why percentage-growth rankings need an absolute-spend or minimum-transaction-count floor to avoid being dominated by noise in tiny categories — this project's ≥20-transaction floor is a direct, disclosed mitigation, not a claim that the problem is fully solved.
- The robustness checks collectively answer the most likely marker challenge to an unsupervised, threshold-dependent pipeline like this one ("why 98th percentile and not some other number, why these exact COVID dates, why this feature set") with concrete sensitivity evidence rather than an unsupported assertion that the choices are reasonable.
- The BI export and dashboard (Phase 7D/7E) demonstrate the pipeline's outputs are not just analytically valid but operationally deliverable — a genuinely novel MSc dissertation contribution beyond the core detection methodology, provided the .pbix/.twbx trade-off is stated plainly rather than presented as if a native BI file had been produced.

## Limitations of the Phase 7 additions (for the dissertation's Limitations section)

- The composite risk score's equal-weighting scheme (1/3 each to anomaly rate, mean anomaly score, rule-flag rate) is a simplifying assumption common in the composite-indicator literature (OECD/JRC, 2008) but not empirically optimised; a weighted or model-learned combination (e.g. via PCA or a supervised proxy target) is a natural extension if ground-truth labels ever become available.
- The category-level pre-COVID → COVID percentage-growth ranking is highly sensitive to the size of the pre-COVID base for small categories; an earlier iteration of this ranking (computed before the Phase 1 data-consolidation fix) surfaced a category with a sub-£1,000 pre-COVID base and a >47,000% swing, illustrating the risk concretely. The ≥20-transaction floor mitigates but does not eliminate this, and an absolute-spend-based secondary ranking should be read alongside the percentage-based one.
- The Phase 7A hub-vs-non-hub composite-score finding and the Phase 6D hub-vs-non-hub raw-anomaly-rate finding measure genuinely different constructs and should not be conflated in the write-up; presenting only one without the other would be misleading.
- The BI-ready CSV/dashboard deliverable is a disclosed, deliberate substitute for a native `.pbix`/`.twbx` file, not a full replacement for one — a marker who specifically requires a native BI-tool file should be pointed to `docs/bi_dashboard_guide.md`, which provides exact field mappings for reconstructing the same dashboard in Tableau Desktop or Power BI Desktop in a few minutes given the exported CSVs.
- The `dim_supplier.csv` community_id/community_size columns are `NaN` for suppliers that were not connected to any other supplier under the Phase 6D co-occurrence projection (a substantial share of the 3,682 risk-scored suppliers) — this is the expected behaviour for an isolated network node, not a data-join defect, but should not be misread as "missing data" when reviewing the export.
