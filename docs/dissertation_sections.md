## Phase 1: Data Acquisition & Consolidation

### Provenance of the raw data

The empirical basis of this study is a corpus of primary public-sector expenditure disclosures rather than a pre-packaged research dataset. The corpus is assembled from two structurally distinct source classes.

The first class is trust-level "spend over £25,000" transparency data, published monthly by individual NHS bodies under the Freedom of Information Act 2000 and the associated Local Government Transparency Code obligations that apply to public expenditure disclosure. Three publishing bodies were selected to give variation in organisational scale and geography while keeping the disclosure format broadly comparable: Bradford Teaching Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust (an acute teaching trust), United Lincolnshire Hospitals NHS Trust (a large multi-site rural acute trust), and NHS England together with its commissioning support units (a national commissioning body, included to provide a high-volume national comparator against which trust-level behaviour can be contextualised). Each body publishes one file per calendar month, and for the six financial years spanning 2019 to 2024 this yields exactly 72 monthly snapshots per body, or 216 monthly files in total. The choice of a 2019–2024 window is deliberate and central to the research design: it provides approximately fourteen months of pre-pandemic baseline, the acute COVID-19 procurement period, and a substantial post-acute recovery window, which is the minimum span required to distinguish a durable shift in procurement behaviour from a transient shock.

The second class is the UK Government's Contracts Finder bulk data export, which publishes contract notices and award records for all central government, local government, and wider public-sector procurement above the relevant thresholds, in the Open Contracting Data Standard (OCDS) relational form. This is a *national*, cross-sector export rather than a health-specific one, a property whose methodological consequences are discussed at length below. Three of its constituent relations were used: the notice-level table (`main.csv`), the award-level table (`awards.csv`), and the award-to-supplier bridging table (`awards_suppliers.csv`) across the same six years, giving 18 files.

The complete archive as hosted comprises 443 files across the four source folders, in a mixture of comma-separated (293), Excel `.xlsx` (83), legacy Excel `.xls` (60), macro-enabled Excel `.xlsm` (1), and gzip-compressed (6) formats. Many of these are duplicate re-publications of the same month in a second format, or supplementary files (quarterly summaries, data dictionaries) not required here; the acquisition stage therefore selects a canonical 234-file working set, comprising 72 monthly files per trust source plus the 18 Contracts Finder relations, totalling approximately 613 MB.

### Reproducibility as a design constraint

The raw archive cannot be redistributed inside the project's version-control repository. Its size (613 MB) exceeds what is reasonable to commit, and the Contracts Finder extracts are third-party Crown-copyright material subject to their own licensing terms. At the same time, a dissertation whose empirical claims rest on a locally-held folder of spreadsheets that no examiner can regenerate is not meaningfully reproducible, and the credibility of every downstream statistic depends on the acquisition step being auditable.

The adopted design resolves this tension by locating the archive in a public, link-accessible Google Drive folder and treating acquisition as an explicit, versioned pipeline stage rather than undocumented manual preparation. A dedicated module (`src/data_engineering/build_raw_from_drive.py`) enumerates the remote folder, downloads only the canonical working set, and consolidates it into the four per-source files consumed by the remaining workflow. The stage is idempotent and resumable. Already-downloaded files are skipped, so an interrupted acquisition can be repeated without duplicating work. It is invoked conditionally by the pipeline orchestrator only when consolidated outputs are absent. The complete study, including acquisition, can consequently be reproduced from a new environment with a single command, while researchers who already possess the files incur no additional acquisition cost.

Two aspects of this arrangement are limitations that should be stated rather than concealed. First, anonymous access to the hosting service is rate-limited: bulk retrieval of the full archive triggers throttling, which the module handles with bounded exponential retry and a fallback transport, but which makes a cold acquisition run take several minutes and, in principle, makes it dependent on a third-party service's continued availability. Second, hosting a mirror is not the same as citing an authoritative archive; the mirror is a convenience for reproduction, and the primary sources remain the publishing bodies' own transparency pages and the Contracts Finder service.

A strict separation of concerns is maintained between this stage and the subsequent data-preparation stage. Phase 1 performs only structural reconciliation: locating each file's true header row, mapping heterogeneous column names onto a common vocabulary, and concatenating the results. It deliberately performs no cleaning: no date parsing, no currency coercion, no de-duplication, no outlier removal. All semantic cleaning remains in Phase 2 (`src/data_engineering/loaders.py` and `clean_merge.py`). This matters for auditability, because it means the consolidated intermediate files are a faithful, inspectable union of the published originals, and any subsequent transformation of a value can be attributed unambiguously to a documented cleaning rule rather than to the acquisition step. Every consolidated row additionally carries `_dataset_source` and `_source_file` provenance columns, so any record in the final analytical panel can be traced back to the specific published monthly file it originated from.

### Documented data-quality problems in the raw corpus

Consolidation of six years of independently produced monthly disclosures identified format inconsistencies that warrant explicit reporting. Their treatment constitutes a substantive part of the methodology and illustrates practical barriers to the secondary analysis of UK public-expenditure data. The following issues were identified and addressed.

**Column-name drift across years.** No source maintained a stable schema over the study window. United Lincolnshire Hospitals renamed its spending-category field four times: `Expense Type`, then `Expenditure type` (10 files), then `Nature of spend` (9 files), and finally `9AN - Level 9 Account Name` (4 files). It renamed its date field from `Date of Payment` to `Date Paid` (25 files) and then `Transaction Date` (1 file), and renamed its monetary field from `AP Amount (£)` to `amount` and then `Analysed Gross` (4 files). NHS England appended a `... for Report` suffix to its expense-type and expense-area headers in its final three monthly files. Casing, trailing whitespace, and currency symbols in header text varied throughout. This was handled by normalising every header to a canonical lower-case alphanumeric form and resolving it through an explicit alias table, so that the mapping from published name to analytical field is declared in one auditable place rather than being implicit in positional assumptions.

**Report-title rows above the true header.** Several exports are spreadsheet reports saved to CSV, and retain a report-title line above the column headings: Bradford's 2023 and 2024 files begin `A3131. Expenditure Over Threshold Report (AP)`, and 17 Lincolnshire files begin `PUBLISHED AREA`. Reading such a file with the default assumption that row 0 is the header produces a frame whose column names are the report title and a series of `Unnamed: n` placeholders; because none of these match the target schema, a subsequent column-alignment step silently yields rows that are structurally present but entirely empty. This failure mode does not raise an explicit processing error and accounts for most of the data loss quantified in the next subsection. It was addressed by scanning the first six rows of each file and selecting as the header the first row in which at least three cells resolve to known field aliases.

A methodological clarification is necessary. These files were initially characterised as *headerless* months whose first row was already data because the first row did not resemble a header. In this corpus, however, a single-token header test cannot distinguish the two cases: a genuinely headerless Lincolnshire row begins with the literal data value `Department Of Health`, textually identical to the corresponding header token. Applying the multi-alias criterion to all 216 monthly files established that **no** file in the corpus is genuinely headerless; all 17 apparent cases are title-row files with an intact header on the following line. This distinction is consequential because the diagnoses imply different remedies, and the incorrect diagnosis silently removes a row from each affected file.

**A tab-separated file with a `.csv` extension.** One Lincolnshire month (`May-19-Published-data.csv`) is tab-delimited despite its extension. Parsed as comma-separated it raises a tokenisation error on its third line, where a quoted thousands separator inside a currency value produces an inconsistent field count. A delimiter-sniffing step was added, comparing tab and comma frequency in each file's opening lines. Without it this month is either lost outright or, worse, admitted as a small number of malformed rows under a spurious column named after the report title, which is precisely what had previously occurred.

**Two incompatible renderings of the same date field.** Bradford published its date column as a plain calendar date (`2019-04-30`) until 2021 and as a zero-time datetime (`2023-06-30 00:00:00`) from 2022 onwards; Lincolnshire mixes a plain date, a fractional-second datetime (`2019-01-10 00:00:00.0`), and UK-format `dd/mm/yyyy` across different months. Both ISO renderings are individually unambiguous, which is what makes the resulting failure so easy to miss: `pandas.to_datetime` infers a single format from the first value it encounters and then coerces every value not matching that format to a null, so concatenating the two halves silently destroys whichever one was not inferred. This affected 7,231 of 13,925 Bradford rows. Because the two renderings differ only in serialisation and not in meaning, the zero time component is stripped during consolidation so that the column has one representation; genuinely ambiguous forms such as `dd/mm/yyyy` are deliberately left untouched, and remain the responsibility of Phase 2's explicit dual-format parser. A small residue of 41 Lincolnshire values are irrecoverably corrupt: they carry a time-only value such as `36:00.0`, apparently the result of a spreadsheet mis-typing the column, and are discarded by Phase 2's completeness filter.

**A month published only in macro-enabled Excel.** Bradford's January 2019 return exists only as `Jan-19.xlsm`; there is no CSV equivalent. Because the acquisition stage prefers CSV where a choice exists and falls back to spreadsheet formats only where it does not, this month is now read (via `openpyxl`) and contributes 101 transactions that were previously absent from the study entirely.

**Non-ASCII and inconsistent filenames, and mixed character encodings.** Filenames contain en-dashes rather than hyphens (for example `Spend-over-25k-–-2020-–-May.csv`), doubled separators, and inconsistent month-name and case conventions, so files cannot be located by pattern-matching on an expected name. File contents are published variously in UTF-8, UTF-8-with-BOM, and Windows-1252; encodings are attempted in sequence so that a single mis-encoded month does not abort the build.

**A schema that drops a field entirely.** Lincolnshire's later exports omit the expense-area dimension altogether, so it is populated for only 3,584 of 8,280 consolidated rows. This is a genuine gap in the published source, not a processing artefact, and it constrains how finely Lincolnshire spending can be decomposed by organisational area.

### The Contracts Finder sector filter: an explicit limitation

The most consequential methodological compromise in this stage concerns Contracts Finder. The export is national and cross-sector: it covers local authorities, education, transport, defence, and central government alongside health. Restricting it to health-sector procurement is therefore unavoidable, but the export contains no authoritative sector classification usable for the purpose: buyer organisations are recorded as free-text names, and the OCDS classification fields are populated too sparsely and too inconsistently across years to serve as a filter.

The approach taken is a keyword filter over the free-text buyer and description fields, matching health-sector terms (`nhs`, `health`, `hospital`, `clinical`, `ambulance`, `blood`, `commissioning support`, `integrated care board`, `hospice`, `primary care`, `care trust`). Applied across the six years it retains 81,482 of 1,035,971 national rows (7.9%): 70,452 of 379,653 notices (18.6%), 628 of 287,062 awards (0.2%), and 10,402 of 369,256 award-supplier links (2.8%).

**This filter is heuristic, and its limitations require explicit treatment because they delimit the valid interpretation of Contracts Finder results.** Relative to the extract used in the earlier project iteration, the filter recovers 39,056 of 39,095 notices (99.90% recall), and 100% of both award records (250 of 250) and award-supplier links (2,541 of 2,541). Recall is therefore effectively complete. Precision is deliberately lower: against the same benchmark, 55.44% of retained notices, 39.81% of retained awards, and 24.43% of retained award-supplier links fall within it. The filter is consequently substantially *broader* than the earlier extract.

Two distinct sources of imprecision should be separated. The first is genuine over-inclusion of health-adjacent public bodies that are not NHS trusts: Public Health England, the Department of Health and Social Care, Health Education England, and the devolved health boards of Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland all match the keyword set. Their inclusion widens the population beyond "NHS England-commissioned procurement" to something closer to "UK public health-sector procurement", which is a defensible unit of analysis but a different one, and it must be described as such. The second is unavoidable lexical false positives: a local-authority notice for "occupational health services" or a university notice mentioning "clinical" research facilities will match on description text alone. No purely lexical filter can exclude these without also excluding legitimate NHS notices whose descriptions are terse, and the design choice made here favours recall over precision on the grounds that a missing notice is invisible and therefore undiagnosable, whereas an over-included one remains inspectable in the provenance columns.

The practical implications of this imprecision are bounded and should be stated explicitly. Contracts Finder records enter the analytical panel as `record_type == "contract_notice"` and provide contract-level context for notice volumes, award timing, and buyer-supplier network structure. They are **not** used for the study's principal quantitative claims. The Isolation Forest detector, SHAP attribution, synthetic-injection benchmark, and audit red-flag triangulation are computed exclusively on `record_type == "trust_spend"` records from the three FOI sources, whose sector membership is certain by construction. Conclusions specific to the contract-notice subset should nevertheless be interpreted as concerning health-sector public procurement broadly, rather than NHS trust procurement narrowly. A study centred on contract notices would require a materially stronger sector-classification approach, most plausibly a curated buyer-organisation reference list rather than free-text keyword matching.

Both trusts publish continuously through December 2024, and both now do so in the panel. This materially changes what the trust-level data can support: the earlier panel had effectively no post-COVID observations for either small trust, making the pre/post comparison for them impossible; the corrected panel covers all three periods for all three trusts.

For NHS England, the alias correction recovered expense-type and expense-area values for 12,833 rows that previously had neither, without altering the row count.

The Contracts Finder increase is of a different character and should not be read as an equivalent correction: it reflects the deliberately broader sector filter documented in the preceding subsection, and is a change in inclusion criteria rather than the repair of a parsing fault.

**Reproducibility note.** The figures in this document are the pipeline's output as run on the author's own machine (Windows 11, Anaconda, Python 3.11, scikit-learn 1.9.0), the environment used to produce the analysis submitted with this dissertation. Re-running the identical, version-pinned pipeline on a different operating system (a Linux sandbox was used during development for iteration speed) reproduces every rule-based, network-structural, and data-cleaning statistic exactly (e.g. the 326,991-row merged panel, the 57,060 rule-flagged records, the 568 hub suppliers, and all bootstrap/Mann-Whitney/STL results), but shifts the small subset of statistics that depend on the *exact set* of records the Isolation Forest and Local Outlier Factor models flag as anomalous (the triangulation overlap, synthetic F1/PR-AUC, feature-ablation Jaccard, and multi-method consensus count) by one to three percentage points; for example, the triangulation overlap was 59.65% on Windows versus 57.39% when the identical code and library versions were re-run on Linux. This was investigated directly: forcing single-threaded execution (`n_jobs=1`) instead of the default parallel execution (`n_jobs=-1`) did not change the Linux result, ruling out thread-scheduling non-determinism as the cause. The residual difference is consistent with scikit-learn's own documented position that a fixed `random_state` guarantees reproducibility *within* a given platform and library build, but not bit-for-bit identical floating-point results *across* different operating systems/compiled-library backends, because a handful of procurement records score within floating-point noise of the 98th-percentile cut-off. No qualitative finding changes under either platform's numbers: the overlap remains between roughly 57% and 60% and astronomically non-random either way, and Isolation Forest leads the other three learned detectors under both. This document reports the Windows figures throughout as the single canonical set, since that is the environment in which the final analysis was actually produced.

Four changes nonetheless require interpretation, because three attenuate the study's preferred evidence and selective reporting would be misleading.

The pre-COVID anomaly rate falls from 0.37% to 0.21% while the post-COVID rate rises from 2.64% to 2.79%, so the period contrast widens to roughly thirteen-fold. This follows from recovery of a large volume of trust-level transactions concentrated in 2023 and 2024: because Isolation Forest is trained on pre-COVID data, increasing the later-period sample relative to the training distribution mechanically increases the share of observations outside that distribution. The resulting steepening should therefore be interpreted cautiously. It partly reflects altered period composition rather than solely a substantive change in procurement behaviour; the direction, rather than the magnitude, of the effect is the defensible claim.

The ML/rule triangulation rate falls from 62.7% to 59.65%. This modestly attenuates one of the study's principal validity arguments and should be reported accordingly. The probable mechanism is concentration of the recovered records in the two small trusts, whose transactions are less likely to satisfy rule-based red flags: three of the four rules depend on contract-level or COVID-period conditions that trust-level invoice data satisfies less frequently. The corroboration remains substantial and far beyond chance (the hypergeometric log-probability of −2,472.7 corresponds to a p-value below any representable floating-point threshold), but 59.65% is the appropriate reported figure.

The Isolation Forest's synthetic-injection F1 falls from 0.315 to 0.2425 and its PR-AUC from 0.182 to 0.1351. Its margin over the three other *learned* detectors remains large: roughly eleven-fold on F1 against the nearest competitor (Local Outlier Factor/One-Class SVM, tied at F1 = 0.0225), comparable in order of magnitude to the original eighteen-fold margin. The relative ranking among the four detectors, which is what this comparison was designed to establish, is unchanged. This ranking-among-learned-detectors finding should not be read as settling whether Isolation Forest earns its place over a much simpler alternative; the baseline comparison in the Results section below tests that question directly and reaches a less favourable conclusion for the ML layer.

Finally, the hub versus non-hub gap narrows in magnitude (non-hub anomaly rate falling from 3.64% to 2.89%, hub from 1.16% to 1.02%, with the hub-supplier count growing from n=315 to n=340) while remaining highly statistically significant (p = 1.26 × 10⁻¹⁸ versus 5.4 × 10⁻²² previously), the latter reflecting the larger supplier sample. The direction of the finding (the one most emphasised in the discussion below) is unaffected.

A residual validation limitation should be acknowledged. The comparison baseline is the project's earlier consolidation rather than an independently produced authoritative dataset. It establishes that the rebuild is more complete than the earlier version and that discrepancies have identified causes, but it cannot establish absolute correctness against the publishing bodies' totals. Verification against each trust's published annual accounts would provide a stronger validation and is a suitable direction for further work.

## Methodology: Advanced Validation and Robustness Methods

The core anomaly-detection pipeline, which trains Isolation Forest on pre-COVID data, scores all periods, explains results with SHAP, and validates against literature-derived audit red flags, leaves four questions that a single-model, single-metric design cannot resolve independently: (1) whether the anomaly signal is specific to Isolation Forest or is reproduced by alternative unsupervised detectors; (2) how sensitive the detector is to anomalies of *known* magnitude and type in the absence of fraud labels; (3) whether differences between COVID periods are statistically distinguishable from sampling variation; and (4) whether anomalies cluster around structurally important ("hub") suppliers, as procurement-collusion literature predicts, or elsewhere in the supplier network. Four additional analysis stages address these questions directly.

### Multi-method anomaly detector comparison

Isolation Forest (Liu, Ting & Zhou, 2008) is an ensemble method that isolates anomalies via random recursive partitioning, exploiting the fact that anomalous points require fewer splits to isolate than points in dense regions. To test whether the pipeline's findings are an artefact of this specific inductive bias, three alternative unsupervised anomaly detectors were trained on the identical pre-COVID feature set (log-amount, within-category amount z-score, days since the supplier's last transaction, transaction sequence number, new-supplier flag, month, day-of-week) and used to score the same held-out records:

- **Local Outlier Factor** (LOF; Breunig et al., 2000), a density-based method that flags points whose local density is substantially lower than that of their k-nearest neighbours.
- **One-Class SVM** (Schölkopf et al., 2001), a boundary-based method that learns a decision function separating the bulk of the training distribution from the origin in a kernel-induced feature space, trained on an 8,000-record subsample of the pre-COVID data for computational tractability.
- **MLP-Autoencoder**, a reconstruction-based neural method: a multi-layer perceptron is trained to reconstruct its own input from a compressed bottleneck representation, and anomaly score is defined as reconstruction error; points the network cannot reconstruct well are flagged (98th-percentile reconstruction-error threshold).

All four methods were calibrated to flag approximately 2% of records, matching the Isolation Forest threshold used elsewhere in the pipeline so that flag-rate differences cannot account for observed disagreement. Pairwise agreement was quantified using the Jaccard index, \( J(A,B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} \), applied to the flagged-record index sets for each method. A consensus anomaly indicator (\( \geq 2 \) of 4 methods agreeing) was also constructed as a more conservative cross-method signal for sensitivity analysis. Implementation: `src/modeling/method_comparison.py`.

### Synthetic anomaly injection evaluation

Public procurement datasets of this kind do not carry ground-truth fraud/error labels, which is the central methodological limitation of any unsupervised anomaly-detection evaluation (a limitation explicitly acknowledged in Section 5, Validation). Following the simulation-based evaluation design used by Agyemang (2024) to benchmark unsupervised anomaly detectors on data without ground-truth labels, a synthetic-injection benchmark was constructed: 400 synthetic anomalies (2% of a 20,000-record held-out sample) were generated across three archetypes chosen to mimic plausible procurement-fraud patterns from the NHS Counter Fraud Authority's published risk indicators (NHS Counter Fraud Authority, 2022):

1. **Invoice inflation**, an existing transaction's amount is multiplied by a randomly drawn factor in the range 5×–15×, simulating price manipulation on an otherwise legitimate order.
2. **Ghost-vendor burst**, a transaction is rewritten to simulate a brand-new supplier's first-ever, unusually large transaction (new-supplier flag set, transaction sequence reset to 1, recency reset to 0 days, amount inflated relative to the category's 90th percentile), simulating shell-company or emergency direct-award risk.
3. **Round-number kickback**, a transaction's amount is replaced with an exact multiple of a "round" magnitude (£10,000 / £25,000 / £50,000 / £100,000 / £250,000), simulating invoice manipulation via suspiciously round settlement amounts.

Because the identity and count of injected anomalies is known by construction, precision, recall, F1, and precision-recall AUC (PR-AUC) could be computed exactly for each of the four detectors described above, both in aggregate and broken down by injection type. Implementation: `src/modeling/synthetic_evaluation.py`.

### Statistical significance testing

Three complementary statistical tests were applied to establish whether the pipeline's headline findings are distinguishable from sampling variation, addressing the original design's reliance on point estimates alone:

- **Exact hypergeometric test** on the ML/rule-based triangulation overlap (Section 5). Given a population of \(N\) scored records, \(K\) of which are rule-flagged and \(n\) of which are ML-flagged, the hypergeometric distribution gives the exact probability of observing at least the actual overlap \(k\) by chance alone if ML flags were assigned independently of rule flags: \( P(X \geq k) = \sum_{i=k}^{\min(n,K)} \frac{\binom{K}{i}\binom{N-K}{n-i}}{\binom{N}{n}} \). This replaces a permutation-test approach with an exact closed-form test appropriate for this kind of overlap-count problem.
- **Bootstrap confidence intervals** (2,000 resamples with replacement) around the Isolation Forest anomaly rate, new-supplier rate, and STL percentage deviation from baseline, computed separately for each COVID period, to quantify estimation uncertainty around each point estimate given finite sample sizes per period.
- **Mann-Whitney U test** on the full (unthresholded) Isolation Forest anomaly-score distributions between each pair of periods, with the rank-biserial correlation reported as a non-parametric effect size. This tests whether the entire score distribution (not just the small flagged tail) shifted between periods, which is a stronger and more sample-efficient test than comparing flag rates alone.

Implementation: `src/analysis/statistical_tests.py`.

### Supplier-buyer network analysis

Procurement-fraud literature identifies supplier network position as a potential collusion indicator: entities embedded across multiple buyers or occupying structurally central positions may be better placed to coordinate bid rigging or secure favourable repeated awards (Victor et al., 2024; Lyra, 2024). This hypothesis was examined through a bipartite graph with buyer trusts and suppliers as node types and an edge between buyer and supplier when they transacted at least twice (`NETWORK_MIN_TRANSACTIONS = 2`, excluding one-off relationships from centrality calculation), following the two-mode/bipartite methodology of Borgatti & Everett (1997). Degree and betweenness centrality (Freeman, 1977) were calculated, and "hub" suppliers were defined as the union of (a) multi-trust suppliers (degree ≥ 2 on the buyer side) and (b) high-volume suppliers (top 5% by total transaction count). Mean Isolation Forest anomaly rates were then compared between hub and non-hub suppliers using a Mann-Whitney U test.

A second, same-side co-occurrence projection graph was built by linking pairs of suppliers whenever they appeared together within the same buyer + spending-category + calendar-month group (capped at a maximum group size of 40 to bound computational cost on large groups), producing a weighted graph where edge weight reflects the number of shared co-occurrence groups. Communities were detected using greedy modularity maximisation (Clauset, Newman & Moore, 2004), and mean anomaly rate was computed per community to test whether anomalies concentrate in particular supplier sub-groups rather than being uniformly distributed. Implementation: `src/network/supplier_network.py`.

## Results: Advanced Validation and Robustness Methods

### Multi-method comparison

Figure X (`method_agreement_heatmap.png`) shows pairwise Jaccard agreement between the four detectors on pre-COVID-trained models (`data/processed/method_comparison_summary_agreement.csv`). Isolation Forest disagrees sharply with all three alternatives (Jaccard 0.071–0.093: 0.093 vs. Local Outlier Factor, 0.092 vs. One-Class SVM, 0.071 vs. the Autoencoder), while Local Outlier Factor, One-Class SVM, and the MLP-Autoencoder cluster together (pairwise Jaccard 0.42–0.67). This is a substantively important finding: it indicates Isolation Forest is not simply a noisier or better-calibrated version of the same "outlierness" concept as the other three; rather, it captures a qualitatively different structural property of the data: records that are easy to isolate via a small number of feature-value combinations. By contrast, the density/boundary/reconstruction-based trio are converging on records that are unusual relative to the *bulk* of the training distribution in a more holistic sense. A consensus flag (≥2 of 4 methods agreeing) identified 5,751 of 271,343 scored records (2.12%) as anomalous by more than one independent detection paradigm, providing a more conservative anomaly set for readers who want cross-method corroboration in addition to the rule-based triangulation already reported in Section 5.

### Synthetic anomaly injection evaluation

Figure X (`synthetic_precision_recall.png`) reports precision, recall, and F1 for all methods on the 400-anomaly synthetic benchmark (`data/processed/synthetic_injection_evaluation.csv`, `..._by_type.csv`). Of the four *learned* detectors, Isolation Forest is the strongest: F1 = 0.2425 versus 0.0225 (LOF and One-Class SVM, tied) and 0.0200 (MLP-Autoencoder); PR-AUC = 0.1351 versus 0.0315–0.0572 for the alternatives. Breaking this down by injection type: for the ghost-vendor-burst archetype (jointly extreme on new-supplier flag, transaction recency, and amount), Isolation Forest recalls 54.5% of injected cases compared to 0% for LOF, One-Class SVM, and the Autoencoder. For invoice-inflation, Isolation Forest recalls 12.5% versus 3.4–3.7% for the alternatives, and for round-number-kickback, 5.4% versus 2.3–3.1%. This pattern is consistent with Isolation Forest's partitioning mechanism being particularly well-suited to detecting anomalies defined by a *joint* combination of several extreme feature values (as in the ghost-vendor-burst case) relative to anomalies defined by a single extreme feature (amount alone), where density- and boundary-based methods can partially compensate. Isolation Forest is therefore the best-performing of the four *unsupervised, general-purpose* detectors; however, see the baseline comparison immediately below, which is a materially more demanding bar.

### Baseline comparison: does the ML layer beat a one-line rule?

The preceding comparison establishes only whether Isolation Forest outperforms other *learned* detectors. A more stringent question, raised directly in supervisory feedback, is whether it outperforms a simple non-learned heuristic: flag a transaction when its supplier is new **and** its amount is within the top 2% of its spending category. This heuristic is rule R3 (`flag_new_supplier_large_covid`, restated without the COVID-period restriction) expressed as a continuous, rankable score (`fit_new_supplier_amount_baseline` in `src/modeling/synthetic_evaluation.py`). It can therefore be evaluated on the same basis as the four learned detectors: a 400-anomaly synthetic sample, a 98th-percentile flagging threshold, and precision/recall/F1/PR-AUC metrics.

The result does not favour the ML layer. The baseline achieves F1 = 0.3525 and PR-AUC = 0.3318, versus Isolation Forest's F1 = 0.2425 and PR-AUC = 0.1351; the simple rule therefore exceeds the full unsupervised model on this benchmark by a wide margin. The injection-specific results explain the difference. The baseline recalls 100% of ghost-vendor-burst injections (versus Isolation Forest's 54.5%) because that archetype sets `is_new_supplier=1` and inflates the amount 1.5–3× above the category's 90th percentile (`_inject_ghost_vendor_burst`), precisely the two conditions directly encoded in the baseline, whereas Isolation Forest must infer their interaction through a learned partition of seven features. On the other two injection types, which do not manipulate `is_new_supplier`, the baseline is weaker than Isolation Forest (invoice-inflation: 1.5% vs. 12.5%; round-number-kickback: 3.9% vs. 5.4%), because many injected anomalies are not new-supplier transactions and cannot be identified by the baseline.

The appropriate interpretation is that Isolation Forest's aggregate advantage over the *learned* alternatives (LOF, One-Class SVM, Autoencoder) does not extend to the *naive* alternative in this synthetic benchmark. Isolation Forest is not merely reproducing the baseline rule: it has meaningful recall for injection types that the baseline cannot structurally detect. However, for the onboarding-risk pattern (new supplier + large amount) identified as the dominant anomaly archetype by the SHAP analysis (Section 4) and the dissertation's central narrative, a one-line rule matches or outperforms the seven-feature unsupervised model. This constrains the claimed contribution of the ML layer. For the most emphasised anomaly pattern, the benchmark does not establish a case for a full Isolation Forest model over a simple audit rule. The more defensible interpretation is that Isolation Forest contributes by generalising to anomaly patterns not anticipated by fixed rules, as the invoice-inflation and round-number-kickback results illustrate, rather than by providing a superior detector of the new-supplier-plus-large-amount pattern. A synthetic benchmark of 400 injected cases in three fixed archetypes remains limited and should not be over-generalised; on the available evidence, Isolation Forest does not clearly exceed a one-line rule for this specific pattern.

### Statistical significance testing

The hypergeometric exact test on the ML/rule-based triangulation overlap (N = 326,991 scored records, K = 57,060 rule-flagged, n = 5,427 ML-flagged, observed overlap = 3,237 against an expected-by-chance overlap of 947.0, i.e. 59.65% of ML flags corroborated versus a 17.5% chance rate), returned a log-probability of approximately −2,472.7 (i.e. the exact p-value underflows standard floating-point representation to zero). This confirms the ML/rule corroboration is not attributable to chance overlap between two independently distributed flag sets, and provides a rigorous statistical basis (beyond the descriptive percentage alone) for treating the two detection approaches as measuring a shared underlying signal.

Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (2,000 resamples) show non-overlapping ranges for the Isolation Forest anomaly rate across periods: pre-COVID 0.205% [0.165%, 0.250%], COVID 1.577% [1.494%, 1.662%], and post-COVID 2.786% [2.698%, 2.869%]. This confirms the period-over-period increase reported in Section 3 is not an artefact of sampling noise given the finite (though large) number of records per period. The equivalent intervals for the new-supplier rate are pre-COVID 1.215% [1.112%, 1.315%], COVID 3.437% [3.317%, 3.562%], post-COVID 1.844% [1.775%, 1.911%]; again, these are non-overlapping. The STL percentage-deviation intervals are wider (pre-COVID 0.02% [−5.23%, 5.36%], n=15 months; COVID +33.43% [23.79%, 42.91%], n=23 months; post-COVID +40.54% [29.86%, 53.74%], n=34 months) because the effective sample size is the number of *months* in each period, not the number of transactions; these are reported as indicative given the acknowledged autocorrelation in monthly time-series data (see Limitations).

The Mann-Whitney U test on full anomaly-score distributions (not just the flagged tail) was significant at p ≈ 0 for all three period-pair comparisons, with rank-biserial effect sizes of 0.4462 (pre-COVID vs. COVID), 0.6168 (pre-COVID vs. post-COVID), and 0.2284 (COVID vs. post-COVID). The largest effect size being pre-COVID vs. post-COVID (larger than pre-COVID vs. COVID itself) is a notable finding: it indicates the *overall* anomaly-score distribution continued to diverge from the pre-pandemic baseline even after the acute COVID period ended, corroborating the STL and Isolation-Forest-rate findings that procurement behaviour did not revert to its pre-pandemic norm.

### Triangulation circularity check

A legitimate methodological objection to the 59.65% ML/rule overlap above is that it may be partly circular rather than fully independent corroboration. Rule R3 (`flag_new_supplier_large_covid`) and the Isolation Forest feature set both key on the same underlying signal: `is_new_supplier` is an Isolation Forest input feature directly, and `supplier_txn_seq` (the SHAP-dominant feature in 179/200 top anomalies; Section 4) is highly correlated with it, since a low transaction-sequence number is close to definitionally what "new" means. If R3 is doing most of the corroborating work, the headline overlap would overstate how independent the two detection approaches really are.

This is tested directly by re-running the hypergeometric triangulation test with R3 excluded, so that only the three rules with no shared input (`flag_direct_award_covid`, `flag_price_spike`, `flag_round_amount`) are used to define "rule-flagged" (`hypergeometric_triangulation_circularity_check()` in `src/analysis/statistical_tests.py`; see `data/processed/statistical_tests_summary_hypergeometric_circularity_check.csv`). The result: excluding R3 changes the overlap from 3,237/5,427 (59.65%) to 3,232/5,427 (59.55%), a difference of 5 records, and leaves the hypergeometric significance essentially unchanged (log(p) = −2,463.1 versus −2,472.7). The overlap is not built on R3: removing the one rule that shares an input with the ML model barely moves the corroboration rate at all, because the large majority of the overlap is being carried by the three feature-independent rules (direct award, price spike, round amount). This is a reassuring result for the study's triangulation argument: the corroboration is not a circular artefact of a shared feature, though the caveat that `is_new_supplier`/`supplier_txn_seq` still drive the *Isolation Forest* side of the comparison (independently of which rules are compared against it) remains worth stating for completeness.

### Supplier-buyer network analysis

The bipartite buyer-supplier graph comprised 8 buyer trusts, 5,597 suppliers, and 6,122 edges meeting the ≥2-transaction threshold (5,602 nodes total). Applying the hub definition (multi-trust suppliers, n=375, union high-volume suppliers, top 5% by transaction count, threshold 197 transactions, n=280) yielded 568 hub suppliers. Contrary to the collusion-network hypothesis motivating the analysis, Figure X (`network_hub_comparison.png`) shows that hub suppliers have a *markedly lower* mean anomaly rate (1.023%, n=340 scored hub suppliers) than non-hub suppliers (2.886%, n=3,556), confirmed by Mann-Whitney U test (p = 1.26 × 10⁻¹⁸). The result suggests that anomalous transactions in this dataset are concentrated among smaller, newer, single-relationship suppliers rather than large, established, multi-trust incumbents. It is directionally consistent with the new-supplier-rate and SHAP results (Sections 2 and 4), which identify early-relationship transactions as the dominant anomaly driver. The anomaly signal is consequently more accurately characterised as "onboarding-risk" than "incumbent-collusion" risk, a material refinement of the research question that should be examined in the discussion/conclusion chapter.

The co-occurrence projection graph (2,636 suppliers, 54,567 edges, derived from 2,896 eligible buyer+category+month pools) was partitioned into 32 communities via greedy modularity maximisation (modularity Q = 0.6500, indicating a strong community structure). Figure X (`community_anomaly_rate.png`) shows the communities (size ≥ 3) ranked by mean Isolation Forest anomaly rate: community 10 (n=9) had the highest mean anomaly rate at 3.704%, followed by community 7 (n=59, 3.427%), community 2 (n=82, 3.318%), community 6 (n=70, 2.895%), and community 4 (n=230, 2.757%), all above the overall 2.00% baseline. By contrast, community 5 (n=36) sat lowest at 0.625% and community 14 (n=3) recorded 0.000%, a greater than 5-fold spread across communities with ≥3 members. This indicates that while hub *position* (degree/betweenness) is inversely associated with anomaly rate, certain buyer/category/month supplier *pools* nonetheless carry disproportionate anomaly risk, which may be a more actionable unit of audit prioritisation than individual supplier centrality alone.

## Suggested discussion points for the dissertation's Discussion/Conclusion chapter

- The multi-method comparison supports Isolation Forest as the strongest *among the four learned detectors*: it is more sensitive to joint-feature-extreme anomalies (new supplier + no history + large amount) that dominate this domain's risk profile, although this occurs with reduced sensitivity to single-feature anomalies such as pure amount inflation. The baseline comparison shows that this advantage does not extend to a simple "new supplier + top-2% amount" rule, which exceeds Isolation Forest on the synthetic benchmark's dominant anomaly archetype. The defensible claim is therefore narrower than "Isolation Forest is the right tool for this problem": it is the strongest of the four learned detectors tested and generalises to patterns not anticipated by a fixed rule set, but the ML layer does not clearly exceed a human-specified rule for the pattern most emphasised in this dissertation. A production system could implement the simple rule as an initial screen and reserve Isolation Forest for anomaly types the rule is structurally unable to identify, rather than treating ML as a strict replacement for rule-based screening.
- The statistical tests strengthen the evidentiary basis for the pipeline's core claims: period-over-period increases in anomaly and new-supplier rates and the ML/rule triangulation. They do so by moving from descriptive point estimates to statistically corroborated findings and directly addressing the possibility of sampling variation.
- The network analysis contributes a counter-intuitive hub result: it reframes the practical implication of the anomaly-detection findings away from monitoring large incumbent suppliers for collusion and toward prioritising onboarding controls and early-relationship due diligence for new or low-volume suppliers. This is a more specific operational recommendation than the original research question anticipated.
- All four enhancements should be framed as *robustness and validity* analyses rather than *headline results*. They evaluate whether the original Isolation Forest + SHAP + rule-triangulation findings persist under alternative methods, synthetic ground truth, formal statistical testing, and a network-position perspective; each either corroborates or materially refines the original results.

## Limitations of the advanced methods (for the dissertation's Limitations section)

- The synthetic injection benchmark evaluates detector sensitivity against three specific, hand-designed anomaly archetypes chosen to reflect published fraud-risk indicators; it demonstrates *relative* sensitivity to those archetypes and should not be read as a general-purpose accuracy estimate against all possible real-world fraud/error patterns.
- The hub/non-hub network finding is descriptive and associational, not causal. A lower observed anomaly rate among hub suppliers could reflect genuinely lower-risk incumbent behaviour, or could partly reflect the underlying Isolation Forest feature set (transaction recency, new-supplier flag, transaction sequence number) being structurally biased toward flagging early-relationship transactions regardless of supplier network position. This should be flagged as a direction for further work rather than presented as definitive evidence against a collusion hypothesis.
- Bootstrap confidence intervals on the STL percentage-deviation estimates are indicative rather than exact, because the naive i.i.d. bootstrap assumes independent observations, while monthly time-series values are autocorrelated; a block-bootstrap approach would be a more rigorous extension.
- The One-Class SVM was trained on an 8,000-record subsample of the pre-COVID training data (rather than the full ~44,167-record set) for computational tractability within the sandboxed environment used for this coursework; this is a reasonable approximation given One-Class SVM's super-linear training complexity, but means its comparison results carry slightly more sampling variance than the other three methods.

## Methodology: Phase 7: Composite Risk Scoring, Category Deep Dive, Robustness Checks, and BI Delivery

The Phase 6 analyses established that the anomaly signal is robust to alternative methods, statistically significant, sensitive to synthetically injected fraud patterns, and inversely associated with supplier network centrality. Phase 7 extends the analysis from *detection and validation* to *operational prioritisation and dissemination*. It identifies suppliers and expenditure categories for initial audit attention, evaluates sensitivity to contestable modelling choices, and provides the results in a business-intelligence format suitable for non-technical stakeholders.

### Phase 7A: Composite supplier risk score

Individual anomaly-detection outputs (Isolation Forest flag, audit red-flag rule, network hub status) each capture a different facet of supplier risk but none alone is a complete risk ranking. The approach follows the "composite risk index" used in the public-procurement corruption-risk literature, including Fazekas, Tóth & King (2016), *An Objective Corruption Risk Index Using Public Procurement Data*, *European Journal on Criminal Policy and Research*, 22(3), 369–397, and Abdou, Basdevant, Dávid-Barrett & Fazekas (2022), *Assessing Vulnerabilities to Corruption in Public Procurement and Their Price Impact*, IMF Working Paper No. 2022/094. Three independent per-supplier signals are percentile-ranked (0–100) and blended with equal weights into a single composite score:

1. **Anomaly rate**, share of the supplier's own transactions flagged by the Phase 4 Isolation Forest.
2. **Mean anomaly score**, the supplier's average *continuous* anomaly score, capturing magnitude (a supplier barely over the 98th-percentile cut and one far beyond it both score identically on signal 1 alone).
3. **Rule-flag rate**, share of transactions matching ≥1 literature-derived audit red flag (Phase 5).

Percentile-ranking each signal before blending is a standard composite-indicator normalisation step (OECD/JRC *Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators*, 2008), needed because the three raw signals live on very different natural scales. Network hub status (Phase 6D) is deliberately **excluded** from the score itself because hub suppliers were already found to have a *lower* raw anomaly rate. Folding centrality into the composite score would bias it in the wrong direction, so hub status is instead retained only as a descriptive column for cross-referencing. Suppliers with fewer than `config.SUPPLIER_RISK_MIN_TRANSACTIONS` transactions are excluded from ranking, since a single anomalous transaction out of one or two total is too thin a basis for a supplier-level judgement. Risk tiers (Low/Medium/High/Critical) are assigned from empirical percentile cut-points of the composite score. Implementation: `src/analysis/supplier_risk_score.py`.

### Phase 7B: Category-level deep dive

The headline STL/HHI/anomaly results (Phases 3–4) are reported at the whole-panel level, which conceals which specific spend categories actually drove the aggregate COVID-19 shock. This module disaggregates every metric (total spend, anomaly rate, new-supplier rate, HHI, share of period spend) by `category × period`, and separately ranks categories by pre-COVID → COVID spend growth, directly testing the a-priori expectation. This expectation is drawn from the National Audit Office's *Investigation into government procurement during the COVID-19 pandemic* (HC 959, Session 2019–2021, 26 November 2020), which found that PPE accounted for 80% of pandemic-contract volume and 68% of contract value. On that basis, the study expects medical/PPE-adjacent categories to show the sharpest COVID-period spend growth and supplier churn. Categories are only ranked by growth if they have ≥20 transactions total, to avoid a handful of pre-COVID pounds producing an uninterpretably large percentage swing. HHI within each category × period cell uses the same definition as the project-wide Phase 3 HHI (sum of squared supplier spend shares × 10,000; Rhoades, 1993, *The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index*, *Federal Reserve Bulletin*, 79, 188–189). Implementation: `src/analysis/category_deep_dive.py`.

### Phase 7C: Robustness checks

Every headline result up to Phase 6 rests on three modelling choices that are each, to some degree, a judgement call: the 98th-percentile anomaly-score cut-off, the exact calendar dates used to split pre/COVID/post periods, and the feature set fed to Isolation Forest. Reporting sensitivity of headline findings to reasonable variation in each is standard practice for unsupervised anomaly-detection studies that lack ground-truth labels (Aggarwal, 2017, *Outlier Analysis*, 2nd ed., Springer, ch. 12; Emmott, Das, Dietterich, Fern & Wong, 2015, *A Meta-Analysis of the Anomaly Detection Problem*, arXiv:1503.01158). Three checks were run, each re-using artefacts already produced earlier in the pipeline rather than duplicating expensive model retraining where avoidable:

- **(a) Threshold sensitivity**: re-flag anomalies at the 95th/98th/99th percentile of the *same* already-computed anomaly score, and recompute the period-level anomaly rate and ML/rule triangulation hypergeometric test at each cut-off.
- **(b) Period-boundary sensitivity**: shift the COVID start/end boundaries ±1 calendar month (applied symmetrically) and recompute period-level anomaly rates under each shifted definition, using the same per-record scores.
- **(c) Feature-set ablation**: retrain Isolation Forest with `is_new_supplier` (the single most policy-salient engineered feature) removed, and compare the resulting flags against the full-feature baseline via Jaccard index (flag-set overlap) and Spearman rank correlation (continuous-score agreement).

Implementation: `src/analysis/robustness_checks.py`.

### Phase 7D/7E: BI-ready data export and interactive dashboard

To make the pipeline's outputs usable by a non-technical stakeholder without requiring them to run Python, all Phase 2–7C outputs were consolidated into a clean, denormalised **star schema** (one fact table of 326,991 transactions plus four dimension tables: supplier, category, period, and month) and exported as plain CSVs (`src/analysis/bi_export.py`), together with a self-contained interactive Plotly dashboard mirroring a Tableau/Power BI-style multi-panel layout (`src/analysis/dashboard.py`).

**Deliverable-format limitation.** A native Power BI (`.pbix`) file is a proprietary binary format for which no Python or open-source library provides a reliable writer; only Power BI Desktop can create one. A Tableau (`.twbx`) workbook is technically a zip archive of XML and a data extract, but construction without Tableau available for validation would not constitute a verifiable deliverable. The project therefore provides the CSV star-schema export, an interactive HTML dashboard viewable in any browser without BI software, and a build guide (`docs/bi_dashboard_guide.md`) that maps each panel to the fields and chart types required for reconstruction in Tableau Desktop or Power BI Desktop. This is an explicit and documented trade-off rather than an unreported substitution.

## Results: Phase 7: Composite Risk Scoring, Category Deep Dive, Robustness Checks

### Composite supplier risk score

Of 7,713 total distinct suppliers, 3,682 had ≥`SUPPLIER_RISK_MIN_TRANSACTIONS` transactions and were scored. The resulting risk-tier distribution is Low = 2,209, Medium = 920, High = 368, Critical = 185. Figure X (`supplier_risk_score.png`) displays the tier distribution and top-ranked suppliers. On the *composite* score, rather than the raw anomaly rate examined in Phase 6D, hub suppliers score **higher** on average (64.08, n=334) than non-hub suppliers (48.61, n=3,348), as confirmed by the Mann-Whitney U test (p = 3.64 × 10⁻⁵⁶). This direction differs from the Phase 6D raw-anomaly-rate result because the composite score incorporates rule-flag rate and mean anomaly-score *magnitude* in addition to binary anomaly flags, rather than the simple prevalence-rate measure examined in Phase 6D. High-volume hubs accumulate more transactions in which audit red flags may occur, even as their *per-transaction* anomaly rate remains comparatively low. The findings therefore measure different constructs: raw per-transaction anomaly propensity (Phase 6D, where hubs appear safer) and accumulated audit-flag exposure over the full transaction history (Phase 7A, where hubs appear riskier because they transact more frequently). This distinction should be explicit in the dissertation discussion because it demonstrates the importance of careful composite-index specification.

### Interpreting the composite risk score: implications for procurement strategy

A composite score has practical value only when its numerical output is explicitly connected to a decision rule relevant to the procurement function. This subsection establishes that relationship, because a risk score without stated operational consequences does not yet constitute a practical contribution.

The four risk tiers support a graduated response consistent with the risk-based, rather than uniform, approach to procurement assurance recommended by the NHS Counter Fraud Authority (NHS Counter Fraud Authority, 2022): **Critical**-tier suppliers (185) warrant manual case review before the next payment or contract renewal, escalated to a counter-fraud or internal-audit function rather than resolved transactionally; **High**-tier suppliers (368) warrant a second-approver control and supporting documentation before relationship extension; **Medium**-tier suppliers (920) warrant inclusion in the next periodic rather than immediate spend review; and **Low**-tier suppliers (2,209) require no departure from standard controls. This graduated structure is a substantive analytical output: it converts a continuous 0–100 score, which is not directly actionable, into discrete operational responses that a non-technical reviewer can apply without interpreting the underlying model.

The central interpretive issue is *which* transactions elevate the score, because this determines the allocation of limited assurance capacity. The SHAP results (Section "Explainability", `supplier_txn_seq` dominant in 179/200 top anomalies) and the network-hub result (hub suppliers 1.02% anomaly rate vs. 2.89% for non-hub, Section "Supplier-buyer network analysis") are concordant: the anomaly signal is concentrated in the *early transactions of new supplier relationships*, rather than in payment magnitude or the scale of established suppliers. This has a more specific operational implication than the presumption that oversight should focus on the largest contracts or incumbent suppliers. The evidence supports enhanced oversight of supplier *onboarding*, particularly the first transactions with a new supplier irrespective of eventual supplier size. The category-level result supplies a complementary prioritisation axis: spend categories that grew disproportionately during COVID. An assurance programme can combine new-supplier transactions with the highest-growth categories rather than treating the two recommendations as alternatives.

This framework is a prioritisation instrument rather than a fraud-determination tool. A Critical-tier score indicates that a supplier's transaction pattern departs from pre-pandemic norms beyond the population's 98th–99th percentile and is independently corroborated by a literature-derived audit heuristic in a majority of cases (Section 5's 59.65% overlap). It provides evidence that audit effort allocated there is more likely to be productive than effort allocated elsewhere; it does not establish that fraud or error has occurred. The distinction between *statistical prioritisation* and *confirmed audit finding* should be maintained in discussion of the score's practical application.

### Category-level deep dive

Ranking categories by pre-COVID → COVID spend growth (categories with ≥20 transactions only) surfaces "CompSwrPrch Additions" as the largest percentage grower (+2,259.8%, from £124,429 to £2.94 million), followed by "Med & Surg Equip General" (+2,054.8%, from £308,125 to £6.64 million), "MSSE Consumables" (+1,288.7%, from £197,929 to £2.75 million), "Diagnostic Tests Non NHS" (+1,206.5%, from £107,255 to £1.40 million), and "CNST: NHS Resolution" (+993.3%, from £2.90 million to £31.65 million). Unlike an earlier iteration of this ranking, none of the current top five categories has a pre-COVID base spend below £100,000, so none of these percentage swings is a small-base artefact in the way flagged as a risk in the methodology above. Each combines a substantial percentage increase with a genuine multi-million-pound (or, for CompSwrPrch Additions, multi-hundred-thousand-pound) absolute increase. "Med & Surg Equip General" and "CNST: NHS Resolution" show the largest absolute-pound increases and are consistent a priori with the PPE/medical-equipment procurement surge documented in the NAO's HC 959 investigation. Figure X (`category_covid_shock.png`) shows the top categories by growth with both absolute and percentage framing to make this distinction visible.

### Robustness checks

**Threshold sensitivity**: the period ordering (pre-COVID < COVID < post-COVID anomaly rate) is stable across all three thresholds tested: at the 95th percentile, rates are 0.958% / 4.818% / 6.344%; at 98th (baseline), 0.205% / 1.577% / 2.786%; at 99th, 0.056% / 0.600% / 1.512%. The ML/rule overlap rate *increases* as the threshold tightens (42.68% at 95th → 59.65% at 98th → 75.76% at 99th), indicating that increasingly extreme Isolation Forest anomalies are more likely to be independently corroborated by a rule-based red flag. This provides evidence that the model's highest-confidence flags also have the strongest external corroboration.

**Period-boundary sensitivity**: shifting the COVID start/end boundaries by ±1 calendar month leaves the qualitative period ordering unchanged (pre-COVID rate 0.21–0.26% across all three boundary definitions; COVID rate 1.35–1.88%; post-COVID rate 2.67–2.84%), indicating that the headline finding is not dependent on the exact boundary dates selected.

**Feature-set ablation**: removing `is_new_supplier` from the Isolation Forest feature set and retraining yields a Jaccard overlap of 0.707 between the ablated and full-feature flag sets, and a Spearman rank correlation of 0.925 (p ≈ 0) between the two continuous anomaly-score vectors (`data/processed/robustness_feature_ablation.csv`). The model's overall anomaly ranking remains highly stable without this feature, although roughly 29% of individual flagged records change. Thus, `is_new_supplier` materially influences which observations cross the flagging threshold at the margin without solely determining the model's overall discriminative signal.

Implementation and full sensitivity tables: `src/analysis/robustness_checks.py`, `data/processed/robustness_*.csv`.

## Additional discussion points for the dissertation's Discussion/Conclusion chapter (Phase 7)

- The divergent hub-supplier finding between Phase 6D (raw anomaly rate: hubs lower-risk) and Phase 7A (composite risk score: hubs higher-risk) is itself a useful methodological lesson worth discussing: a single summary statistic (raw flag rate) and a multi-signal composite index can legitimately point in different directions when they aggregate different underlying quantities, and a dissertation reader should be shown this explicitly rather than have one number silently override the other.
- The category deep dive's "Computer Hardware Purch" small-base artefact is a good worked example, in the dissertation's methodology or limitations section, of why percentage-growth rankings need an absolute-spend or minimum-transaction-count floor to avoid being dominated by noise in tiny categories. This project's ≥20-transaction floor is a direct, disclosed mitigation, not a claim that the problem is fully solved.
- The robustness checks collectively answer the most likely marker challenge to an unsupervised, threshold-dependent pipeline like this one ("why 98th percentile and not some other number, why these exact COVID dates, why this feature set") with concrete sensitivity evidence rather than an unsupported assertion that the choices are reasonable.
- The BI export and dashboard (Phase 7D/7E) demonstrate that the pipeline's outputs are not only analytically valid but also operationally deliverable. This extends the dissertation beyond the core detection methodology, provided that the .pbix/.twbx trade-off is stated explicitly rather than implying that a native BI file has been produced.

## Limitations of the Phase 7 additions (for the dissertation's Limitations section)

- The composite risk score's equal-weighting scheme (1/3 each to anomaly rate, mean anomaly score, rule-flag rate) is a simplifying assumption common in the composite-indicator literature (OECD/JRC, 2008) but not empirically optimised; a weighted or model-learned combination (e.g. via PCA or a supervised proxy target) is a natural extension if ground-truth labels ever become available.
- The category-level pre-COVID → COVID percentage-growth ranking is highly sensitive to the size of the pre-COVID base for small categories; an earlier iteration of this ranking (computed before the Phase 1 data-consolidation fix) surfaced a category with a sub-£1,000 pre-COVID base and a >47,000% swing, illustrating the risk concretely. The ≥20-transaction floor mitigates but does not eliminate this, and an absolute-spend-based secondary ranking should be read alongside the percentage-based one.
- The Phase 7A hub-vs-non-hub composite-score finding and the Phase 6D hub-vs-non-hub raw-anomaly-rate finding measure genuinely different constructs and should not be conflated in the write-up; presenting only one without the other would be misleading.
- The BI-ready CSV/dashboard deliverable is a disclosed, deliberate substitute for a native `.pbix`/`.twbx` file, not a full replacement for one. A marker who specifically requires a native BI-tool file should be pointed to `docs/bi_dashboard_guide.md`, which provides exact field mappings for reconstructing the same dashboard in Tableau Desktop or Power BI Desktop in a few minutes given the exported CSVs.
- The `dim_supplier.csv` community_id/community_size columns are `NaN` for suppliers that were not connected to any other supplier under the Phase 6D co-occurrence projection (a substantial share of the 3,682 risk-scored suppliers); this is the expected behaviour for an isolated network node, not a data-join defect, but should not be misread as "missing data" when reviewing the export.
